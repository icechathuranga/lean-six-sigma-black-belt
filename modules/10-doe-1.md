# Module 10 · Design of Experiments I (Weeks 20–21)

## Why it matters

Regression (Module 08) finds what the process *already* shows; a multi-vari (Module 09) narrows where to look. But both are *observational* — they cannot prove that changing an X changes the Y, because something else may move with it. **Design of Experiments (DOE)** is the only tool that establishes cause: you deliberately *set* the factors, run the process, and measure the response, so the differences you see must be due to what you changed. For our running example, the question is sharp: *of three proposed process changes — a structured interview guide, a CV pre-screen, and a scheduling tool — which actually cut time-to-hire, by how much, and do they help more or less when combined?* A full factorial answers all of that from a handful of planned runs.

Work the real dataset `datasets/doe-factorial.csv` with this module: a 2³ full factorial with 2 replicates (16 runs). **DOE analysis is far easier in a stats tool than a spreadsheet** — we show both.

## The vocabulary of a designed experiment

**The intuition.** A DOE is just a disciplined way of trying combinations. The words:

- **Factor** — an input you deliberately set (the structured guide; the pre-screen; the scheduling tool).
- **Level** — a setting of a factor. Here each factor is on/off, so two levels: without / with.
- **Response** — the output you measure (time_to_hire_days).
- **Run (trial)** — one execution of the process at one specified combination of factor levels.
- **Randomisation** — doing the runs in random order so unknown drifts (a quiet hiring week, a new recruiter) don't line up with your factors.
- **Replication** — genuinely repeating whole runs (here 2 reps of each combination) to estimate the natural noise and test significance.
- **Blocking** — grouping runs that share a nuisance condition (e.g. region, or week) so that known, uncontrolled variation is removed from the comparison rather than confounding it.

**The rigorous method.** Formally, factors are the controlled independent variables, each at defined levels; the response is the dependent variable. **Randomisation** protects against bias from lurking variables by making their influence random noise rather than systematic error — it validates the independence assumption behind the analysis. **Replication** (true repeats of a run, *not* repeated measurements of one run) provides an independent estimate of experimental error (pure error), which is the yardstick every effect is tested against. **Blocking** removes a known nuisance factor's variation from the error term: you balance each treatment across the blocks, trading a few degrees of freedom for a more sensitive test. The three are a package — *block what you can, randomise against what you can't, replicate to measure what's left.*

## Why DOE beats one-factor-at-a-time

**The intuition.** The tempting approach is **one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT)**: hold everything fixed, change the guide, see what happens; then fix that and change the pre-screen; and so on. OFAT has two fatal flaws. First, it is *inefficient* — you spend runs learning about each factor in isolation. Second, and worse, it is *blind to interactions*: if the guide helps more when the pre-screen is off, OFAT — which only ever varies one thing from one baseline — can never see it. A factorial varies everything together in a balanced pattern, so every run contributes to *every* factor's estimate and to the interactions.

**The rigorous method.** In a 2^k factorial each main effect is estimated from *all* 2^k runs (the full design is "hidden-replicated" for main effects), giving the same precision as OFAT for far fewer runs, *and* it estimates interactions OFAT cannot reach. OFAT also explores only a cross-shaped slice of the factor space and can stall at a false optimum when factors interact. The factorial spans the corners of the space, so it detects interactions and supports a valid regression model (detecting *curvature* needs centre points — Module 11). Efficiency and interaction-detection together are why DOE is the Black-Belt standard and OFAT is discouraged.

## The full factorial 2^k, in coded and actual units

**The intuition.** "2^k" means k factors each at 2 levels, run at *every* combination: with 3 factors that is 2³ = 8 combinations, and with 2 replicates, 16 runs. We write each factor in **coded units**: −1 for the low level, +1 for the high level. In this dataset we take **−1 = without the change, +1 = with it** (the CSV stores only ±1, so this coding is our stated convention). Coded units make the design symmetric and the maths clean; **actual units** are the real-world settings the codes stand for (guide absent/present, etc.). The eight corners, each run twice, let you estimate the grand mean, three main effects, three two-factor interactions and one three-factor interaction.

**The rigorous method.** The design matrix lists the ±1 pattern for A, B, C and derives each interaction column by multiplying its parent columns (A×B column = product of A and B rows). All columns are **orthogonal** (balanced and uncorrelated), so every effect is estimated independently of the others — a defining strength of factorials. Fitting the regression in coded units, the **intercept equals the grand mean** (here **30.04 days**, the centre of the data) and each **coded coefficient = effect ÷ 2**, because the factor travels 2 coded units from −1 to +1. To convert to actual units, substitute the real low/high values; coded analysis is preferred for interpreting and comparing effects because the coefficients are then on a common, scale-free footing.

## Main effects, interactions, and their plots

**The intuition.** A **main effect** is how much the response changes, on average, when you switch a factor from low to high. From the data:

| Factor (−1 → +1) | Mean at −1 | Mean at +1 | **Effect** | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A structured_guide | 33.21 | 26.88 | **−6.34** | significant |
| B prescreen | 31.41 | 28.67 | **−2.74** | significant |
| C scheduling_tool | 30.78 | 29.30 | **−1.48** | significant |

So the structured guide is the heavy hitter — on its own it cuts time-to-hire by **6.34 days**; the pre-screen saves **2.74**; the scheduling tool **1.48**. (All effects negative = every change reduces days, good for a smaller-is-better response.)

An **interaction** is when the effect of one factor *depends on the level of another*. The **A×B interaction effect is +1.43** and significant. Because the response is smaller-is-better, a *positive* interaction here is **antagonistic**, not synergistic: the guide saves **7.76 days when the pre-screen is off** but only **4.91 days when it is on** — the two partly do the same job, so stacking them gives less than the sum of their solo savings. That is a real planning insight: budget for diminishing returns if you deploy both.

**The rigorous method.** Main effect of A = ȳ(A=+1) − ȳ(A=−1), averaged over all other factors — which orthogonality guarantees is unconfounded. An interaction effect A×B = ½[(effect of A when B=+1) − (effect of A when B=−1)] = ½[(−4.91) − (−7.76)] = **+1.43**. Each effect is tested against pure error from the replicates; here A (p ≈ 1.6×10⁻⁶), B (p = 0.00067), C (p = 0.020) and A×B (p = 0.023) are all significant at α = 0.05, while A×C (p = 0.82) and the three-way A×B×C (p = 0.47) are not. **B×C (p = 0.0502) is borderline** — it just misses α = 0.05, so treat it as "worth a closer look," not a clean null. The model fits well: **R² = 0.963**.

*Reading the plots.* **The intuition:** a **main-effects plot** draws the response mean at each factor's low and high level and joins them — a *steeper* line means a bigger effect, so A's line is much steeper than C's. An **interaction plot** draws the response against one factor with a separate line per level of the second; **parallel lines = no interaction, non-parallel lines = interaction**. **The rigorous method:** for A×B, plot mean response versus A with one line for B=−1 and one for B=+1; the lines are non-parallel (they converge) — the B=−1 line falls 7.76 days across A while the B=+1 line falls only 4.91, so the vertical gap between them narrows from 4.16 to 1.31 days. The half-difference of those slopes, ½[(−4.91) − (−7.76)] = +1.43, is the interaction made visible. Always interpret a significant interaction *before* its parent main effects, because the main effect is then an average across conditions that behave differently.

## Common pitfalls

- **Calling a positive interaction "synergy."** On a smaller-is-better response, +1.43 is *antagonistic* — combined savings are less than the sum. Always check the direction against what "good" means.
- **Reading main effects while ignoring a significant interaction.** A's "−6.34" is an average of −7.76 and −4.91; quote the conditional effects when the interaction matters.
- **Confusing replication with repeated measurement.** Re-reading one run doesn't estimate experimental error; you must repeat the whole run.
- **Skipping randomisation.** Running all "with-guide" trials first lets a time drift masquerade as the guide's effect.
- **Treating p = 0.0502 as a firm "no".** B×C is borderline, not disproven — flag it for a follow-up rather than discarding it.
- **Hand-testing significance.** The arithmetic is error-prone; use a stats tool for the p-values and keep the spreadsheet for the effect sizes and plots.

## Self-check

1. **Q:** How many runs are in a 2³ full factorial with 2 replicates, and what does replication buy you? **A:** 8 combinations × 2 = 16 runs. Replication gives an independent estimate of experimental (pure) error, which is needed to test each effect for significance.
2. **Q:** The coded intercept is 30.04 and A's coded coefficient is −3.17. Relate these to the data. **A:** The intercept is the grand mean (centre of the design); the coded coefficient is half the effect, so A's effect = 2 × (−3.17) = −6.34 days.
3. **Q:** A×B = +1.43 and the response is time-to-hire. Synergy or antagonism, and what does it mean operationally? **A:** Antagonism — the guide saves 7.76 days alone but only 4.91 with the pre-screen, so deploying both gives diminishing returns.
4. **Q:** Why does DOE beat OFAT here? **A:** OFAT cannot detect the A×B interaction and uses runs less efficiently; the factorial estimates every main effect from all 16 runs *and* reveals the interactions.
5. **Q:** C is significant (p = 0.020) but B×C is p = 0.0502. How do you report each? **A:** C is a real, if small, main effect (−1.48 days). B×C just misses α = 0.05 — report it as borderline and worth a targeted follow-up, not as established or as null.
