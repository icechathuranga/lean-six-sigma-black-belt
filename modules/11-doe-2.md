## Why it matters

A full factorial (Module 10) is wonderful until the factor list grows: 2⁵ is 32 runs, 2⁷ is 128, and each run is a real cohort of hires you cannot re-run cheaply. Black-Belt DOE therefore spends runs *strategically* — screen many factors with few runs, confirm the vital few, then optimise. This module covers the tools that make that possible: **fractional factorials**, **screening designs**, **centre points** to catch curvature, and **response surface methods** to find the best settings. The thread remains reducing time-to-hire. We use the real 2³ full factorial in `datasets/doe-factorial.csv` as the honest baseline against which a *fraction* is judged; numbers introduced for curvature and optimisation are **explicitly illustrative**, because those methods need continuous factors our on/off dataset doesn't have. As in Module 10, **the analysis is far easier in a stats tool than a spreadsheet.**

## Fractional factorials: generators, aliasing, resolution

**The intuition.** A fractional factorial runs only a carefully chosen *fraction* of the full set — a 2^(k−p) design uses 1/2ᵖ of the runs. The saving is not free: by running half the corners you lose the ability to tell certain effects apart, and they become **confounded** (aliased) — their estimates add together. We can show this *exactly* from the real data. Take the 2³ in `doe-factorial.csv` and keep only the four corners where A×B×C = +1 (a half fraction, 2³⁻¹). Re-estimate the main effects from that half:

| Effect from half fraction | Estimate | What it is really measuring |
|---|---|---|
| A | **−7.51** | A + BC |
| B | **−2.85** | B + AC |
| C | **−0.05** | C + AB |

Compare with the full factorial (Module 10): the true C effect is **−1.48** and the A×B interaction is **+1.43**. In the half fraction they are *aliased*, so what you measure is their sum: −1.48 + 1.43 = **−0.05**. The half-fraction experiment would have told you the scheduling tool does *nothing* — a false conclusion, caused entirely by C being confounded with the A×B interaction. That is the central lesson of fractional designs: you buy fewer runs with the risk of tangled effects.

**The rigorous method.** You build the fraction with **generators**. For a half fraction of three factors, set the generator **C = AB**; equivalently the **defining relation** is **I = ABC** (the identity column equals the ABC product). Multiplying through by each term gives the **alias structure**: A = BC, B = AC, C = AB (because any column squared = I). **Resolution** is the length of the shortest "word" in the defining relation — here ABC has length 3, so this is **Resolution III**:

- **Resolution III** — main effects aliased with two-factor interactions (cheapest, riskiest; our example).
- **Resolution IV** — main effects clear of two-factor interactions, but 2FIs aliased with each other (good for screening).
- **Resolution V** — main effects and 2FIs all clear of one another (good for modelling).

Higher resolution needs more runs. Choosing a fraction is choosing which effects you are willing to confound, under the **sparsity-of-effects principle** — the assumption that most high-order interactions are negligible, so aliasing a main effect with a (probably tiny) interaction is usually safe. Our dataset is the exception that proves the rule: the A×B interaction was *not* tiny, so the Resolution III fraction misled us.

## Screening designs and Plackett–Burman

**The intuition.** Early in a project you may have eight or ten candidate factors and no idea which few matter. A **screening design** uses the fewest runs that can still separate the *main effects*, accepting that interactions get muddled. You are not trying to model the process yet — just to sift the vital few factors from the trivial many, so the next experiment can focus on them.

**The rigorous method.** Resolution III fractional factorials are the classic screening tool. **Plackett–Burman (PB)** designs are an alternative for two-level screening in run counts that are multiples of 4 (12, 20, 24, …), letting you screen, say, 11 factors in 12 runs. Their trade-off is **complex partial aliasing**: each main effect is fractionally confounded with *many* two-factor interactions rather than one cleanly. That is acceptable *only* under effect sparsity and only for identifying important main effects — PB designs are poor for estimating interactions, so follow up the survivors with a higher-resolution or full design.

## Centre points and detecting curvature

**The intuition.** A two-level factorial only ever measures the response at the *corners*, so the model it fits is a flat (tilted) plane plus interactions — it literally cannot see a *bend* in the surface. If time-to-hire improves as you add interview rounds up to a point and then worsens, the corners alone will miss that hump. **Centre points** — extra runs at the mid-setting of every factor — let you check: if the average response at the centre differs from the average at the corners, the surface is **curved**. These factors must be continuous (you can set 3 interview rounds, the midpoint of 2 and 4; you cannot set "half a structured guide"), so here we switch to **illustrative quantitative factors**: interview_rounds (low 2, centre 3, high 4) and an offer-response-time target. *All figures in this section are illustrative.*

**The rigorous method.** Add centre-point runs at coded 0 for all factors and test H₀: no curvature by comparing the mean of the centre runs, ȳ_C, with the mean of the factorial corners, ȳ_F. A significant difference (ȳ_C − ȳ_F) indicates **pure quadratic curvature** — the first-order-plus-interaction model is inadequate and you need second-order terms. Centre points also supply an independent estimate of pure error (replicated cheaply at one point) and, placed in a continuous-factor design, cost little. *Illustrative:* if the corners average 30.0 days but five centre runs average 26.5, the 3.5-day gap signals curvature — the planar (corner-only) model is inadequate and a second-order model is needed.

## Response surface methodology and optimisation

**The intuition.** Once you know curvature is present and have narrowed to two or three continuous factors, you want the *settings that minimise* time-to-hire, not just a yes/no on each factor. **Response Surface Methodology (RSM)** fits a smooth curved surface (a quadratic model) over the factor region and finds its low point — like reading a contour map to locate the bottom of a valley. *All numbers illustrative.*

**The rigorous method.** RSM fits a second-order model, ŷ = β₀ + Σβᵢxᵢ + Σβᵢᵢxᵢ² + Σβᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ, which needs more than two levels per factor. The workhorse design is the **central composite design (CCD)**: a two-level factorial (corners) + centre points + **axial/star points** at ±α on each factor axis (for a *rotatable* design α = (2^k)^¼, i.e. 1.414 for two factors, 1.682 for three). The factorial points estimate main effects and interactions, the centre points estimate curvature and pure error, and the axial points estimate the pure quadratic (xᵢ²) terms. You then locate the **stationary point** by calculus (where the surface's slope is zero) and confirm it is a minimum, or follow a **ridge** if the optimum sits outside the region. *Illustrative:* a fitted surface might locate the minimum at 3 interview rounds and a 1.8-day offer SLA, predicting ≈ 24 days — settings you would then confirm with a few validation runs.

## Sequential experimentation strategy

**The intuition.** Never pour the whole budget into one giant experiment. Experiments *teach you what to do next*, so spend in stages: a small screening design first, learn which factors matter, then a sharper design on the survivors, then an optimisation design. Each step is cheap, and you can stop early if the answer is clear or redirect if a surprise appears.

**The rigorous method.** The classic guidance (Box) is to invest only about a **quarter** of your runs in the first design, keeping reserve for follow-up. The canonical sequence is **screen → characterise → optimise**: (1) a Resolution III/IV fractional or Plackett–Burman design to find the vital few factors; (2) a full or higher-resolution factorial *with centre points* to estimate interactions and test for curvature; (3) if curvature is present, augment to a CCD and run RSM to optimise; then (4) confirmation runs. **Fold-over** (adding the mirror-image fraction) de-aliases a Resolution III screen when a confounded main effect looks important — exactly the fix our C-aliased example would need.

## Common pitfalls

- **Trusting a low-resolution fraction when an interaction is large.** Our C estimate (−0.05) is the warning: Resolution III hides a main effect inside a 2FI. Know your alias structure before you believe an effect — or a non-effect.
- **Reporting an aliased estimate as a pure main effect.** "C = −0.05" is really "C + AB"; always state the alias chain.
- **Putting centre points on categorical factors.** On/off factors have no midpoint; curvature checks and CCDs need continuous factors.
- **Using Plackett–Burman to estimate interactions.** PB screens main effects only; its partial aliasing makes interaction estimates unreliable.
- **One big design instead of a sequence.** You lose the chance to learn and redirect; reserve runs for follow-up and fold-overs.
- **Skipping confirmation runs.** An RSM optimum is a model prediction — validate it at the chosen settings before rolling out.

## Self-check

1. **Q:** In the half fraction, C comes out as −0.05 but the full factorial says −1.48. Why the difference? **A:** In the half fraction C is aliased with A×B (generator C = AB, I = ABC), so the estimate is C + AB = −1.48 + 1.43 = −0.05. Only the full design separates them.
2. **Q:** What is the resolution of a design whose defining relation is I = ABC, and what does that resolution mean? **A:** Resolution III (shortest word length 3) — main effects are aliased with two-factor interactions.
3. **Q:** You have 10 candidate factors and want the vital few fast. Which design family, and what's its limitation? **A:** A Resolution III fractional or Plackett–Burman screening design — few runs, but main effects are confounded with interactions, so it screens main effects only, not interactions.
4. **Q:** Your 2^k factorial fits well but you suspect the best setting is in the interior. What do you add, and what does it reveal? **A:** Centre points — comparing their mean with the corner mean tests for curvature; a significant gap means a quadratic model (RSM/CCD) is needed.
5. **Q:** Outline the sequential strategy and why it beats one large experiment. **A:** Screen (fractional/PB) → characterise with a full factorial + centre points → optimise with a CCD/RSM → confirm. Spending in stages lets each experiment inform the next, costs less up front, and lets you redirect when surprises appear.
