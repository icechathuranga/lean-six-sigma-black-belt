Module Seven. Hypothesis Testing in Depth.

This is where a Black Belt earns the title. A sponsor won't fund a change just because the after average looks a little lower. The numbers always jiggle. Hypothesis testing is the discipline that separates a real effect from random noise, attaches a probability to being wrong, and lets you say the region genuinely drives time-to-hire with evidence, not opinion. Choosing the right test, checking its assumptions, and reading the p-value honestly are the core analytical skills of the Analyse phase.

Every hypothesis test runs the same courtroom drama. The null hypothesis is the defendant, presumed innocent. Nothing has changed, the groups are the same, there is no effect. The alternative hypothesis is the prosecution's claim. Something is going on. You never prove the alternative true. You only ask whether the evidence against the null is strong enough to overturn the presumption of innocence. If the data would be genuinely surprising in a world where the null were true, you reject the null.

The p-value measures that surprise. Assuming the null hypothesis is true, how likely is data at least this extreme? A tiny p-value means this result would be a freak coincidence if nothing were really going on, so you stop believing the nothing story. You fix your threshold, called alpha, in advance, usually zero point zero five, and you reject the null when the p-value falls below it. Now, say this carefully, because it's the most misquoted number in statistics. The p-value is not the probability that the null is true. It is not the probability the result was down to luck. And it is not a measure of how big the effect is. It's one thing only: the probability of data this extreme, if the null were true.

Because you're deciding from a sample, you can be wrong in two ways, and they aren't symmetric. A type one error is convicting an innocent defendant, declaring an effect that isn't real, a false alarm. You told the sponsor the change worked when it didn't. A type two error is acquitting a guilty defendant, missing a real effect that was genuinely there. And power, which is one minus the type two error rate, is the chance of catching a real effect when it exists. A test with low power is a smoke detector with a flat battery. Silence isn't the same as safety. Here's the catch: with fixed data you can't drive both errors to zero at once. Tighten alpha to cut false alarms and you raise the miss rate. The one lever that improves both together is more data.

Let's make it concrete with the main worked example. Did a process change lower time-to-hire? We have twenty-five time-to-hire values from before the change and twenty-five from after. Now, a crucial distinction. These are two independent sets of requisitions, different hires, not the same requisitions measured twice. So this is a two-independent-samples problem, and the right tool is the two-sample t-test. If instead they'd been the very same requisitions measured before and after, you'd use a paired t-test, which is more powerful because it removes the requisition-to-requisition variation. But here they're different hires, so we treat them as independent. The null hypothesis is that the before and after means are equal. The alternative is that they differ. We use Welch's version of the t-test as the safe default, because it doesn't assume the two groups have equal variances. The before group averages thirty-one days, the after group averages about twenty-six point six. And the test statistic comes out at t equals two point eight eight, with a p-value of about zero point zero zero six. That's well below zero point zero five, so we reject the null. The roughly four-point-four-day drop is statistically significant. The change plausibly worked. And a rank-based backup test, the Mann-Whitney, agrees with a p-value of about zero point zero one two, which is reassuring because it doesn't lean on normality at all.

But significance is not importance, so always report the effect size too. A four-point-four-day reduction on a thirty-one-day baseline is about a fourteen percent improvement. Practically meaningful, not just statistically detectable. That pairing, a p-value plus an effect size plus a confidence interval, is the honest way to report every result.

Now the test you reach for depends on three questions. What type is the output, how many groups, and what are you comparing? If you're comparing the means of three or more groups at once, you don't run a pile of separate t-tests, you run analysis of variance. It asks whether the variation between the group means is large relative to the variation within the groups, a ratio captured by the F-statistic. In our data, comparing time-to-hire across four regions gives an F of about fifteen and a p-value far below zero point zero five, effectively zero, so the regions genuinely differ. But analysis of variance only tells you that at least one group differs, not which. For that you follow up with a post-hoc test, such as Tukey's, which pins down the specific pairs while controlling for all the comparisons you're making at once. And when both of your variables are categories, like region versus whether an offer was accepted, you use the chi-square test of independence, which compares the counts you observed against the counts you'd expect if the two were unrelated. For the offers, that gives a chi-square of about nine point two on three degrees of freedom and a p-value of about zero point zero three, so acceptance does depend on region, with the West declining offers far more often than the North.

One more family worth knowing. When the data are badly skewed and the sample is too small for the central limit theorem to rescue the average, you switch to the non-parametric tests, which work on ranks and medians and make no normality assumption. The Mann-Whitney stands in for the two-sample t, the Wilcoxon signed-rank for the paired t, and the Kruskal-Wallis for analysis of variance. They cost a little power when the data really are normal, which is why you default to the ordinary test and switch only when you have a reason.

Let's check it stuck. A pause after each question.

Question one. In plain words, what does a p-value of about zero point zero zero six for the before-and-after test actually mean? ... If the change had truly made no difference, data showing a gap this large or larger would turn up only about zero point six percent of the time. That's strong enough evidence to reject the no-difference story.

Question two. The analysis of variance on regions gives a p-value effectively zero. Can you now say which regions differ? ... Not yet. Analysis of variance only says at least one mean differs. You run a post-hoc test, such as Tukey's, to find which specific pairs differ while controlling the overall error rate.

Question three. Define the type one error, the type two error, and power. ... The type one error is declaring an effect that isn't real, a false alarm. The type two error is missing a real effect. Power is the probability of detecting a real effect when it genuinely exists.
