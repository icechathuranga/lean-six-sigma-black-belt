Module Nine. Multi-vari studies and advanced root cause.

By this point in a project you often have the opposite of the problem you started with. Early on, you had no idea what was driving your process. Now, after all the brainstorming and measuring, you have too many suspects. Fifteen possible causes, and nowhere near enough time or money to test them all. Jumping straight into a designed experiment on fifteen factors would be slow and expensive. So before you spend a penny changing anything, there is a cheaper, cleverer step. It is called a multi-vari study, and the whole idea is this. You watch the process as it already runs. You draw a picture of the variation it is already producing. And you let that picture tell you where the big variation lives. Then you take only those few suspects forward into an experiment. It is a funnel. It turns "everything might matter" into "look here first".

Our example in this module is the consistency of interview scores across a multi-region HR operation. A candidate's score should reflect the candidate, not which interviewer happened to re-score them, and not which week it was. If scores wobble for the wrong reasons, every hiring decision downstream inherits that noise.

Here is the key insight. Almost any variation you can measure falls into one of three families, and knowing the family tells you where to look. The first family is within-unit, or positional variation. That is variation inside a single thing. Here it is the same candidate re-scored by two interviewers, getting slightly different marks. If this family is large, your measurement itself is noisy. The second family is between-unit, or piece-to-piece variation. That is the difference from one item to the next, close together in time. Here it is candidate A scoring higher than candidate B this week. Usually this one is the legitimate signal: real differences between real people. The third family is temporal, or over-time variation. That is the difference from one period to the next. Here it is this week's cohort averaging lower than last week's. If this family is large, something is drifting: a tiring panel, a changing applicant pool, a new rubric.

And the whole point is this. Whichever family is tallest decides your next move. A noisy within-unit family sends you to fix the measurement system. A large temporal family sends you hunting for a drift. A dominant between-unit family may mean the process is behaving perfectly well, and the variation you are seeing is real difference that you should not try to remove.

Let me show you with some illustrative numbers. Imagine we re-score each candidate twice, we look at three candidates a week, across three weeks. When we plot it, each candidate becomes a short vertical tick joining their two scores. The height of that tick is the within-candidate family, and here it is small, only about two points. The scatter of candidates within one week is the between-candidate family, and here it is wide: the candidate averages span seven or eight points. Then we connect the three week averages with a line. Those averages come out around seventy-four and a half, then seventy-one, then just under sixty-eight, a clear downward slide of about seven points. That slope is the temporal family. So the eye immediately sees three things: short ticks, wide scatter within each week, and a sliding week line. Between-candidate is tallest, temporal is real, within-candidate is small.

The chart shows you which family is biggest. To put a number on each, we use components of variation. And here is the one rule you must never forget: variances add, standard deviations do not. When we decompose this illustrative data, the within-candidate piece is a variance of about one and a half, roughly seven percent of the total. The between-candidate piece is about thirteen point four, roughly sixty-three percent. And the temporal piece is about six point four, roughly thirty percent. Add those three variances together and you get a total of about twenty-one point three. The total standard deviation is the square root of that total, about four point six. Notice that is smaller than if you had foolishly added the three standard deviations together. You always sum the variances first, then take the square root at the very end.

So what is the story the numbers tell? About sixty-three percent of the score variation is genuine candidate difference. That is good: it means the process actually discriminates between strong and weak candidates. But about thirty percent is week-to-week drift, which should not exist, and is well worth chasing. Only about seven percent is re-scoring noise, so the scoring itself is reasonably repeatable. That is your map. You would not waste experimental runs tweaking the scoring rubric, because that is not where the action is. You would instead bring the time-linked factors, such as panel fatigue, the weekly applicant source, or rota changes, into your experiment. That is how a multi-vari narrows a long list of suspects down to a short, evidence-backed one. But notice what it never does: it changes nothing, so it can never prove cause. It targets an experiment; it does not replace one.

One more distinction that trips people up: nested versus crossed sampling. A factor is nested when each lower unit belongs to exactly one upper unit. Candidate one exists only in week one; a different candidate is in week two. You cannot ask what is the week effect for candidate one, because candidate one never appears in another week. A factor is crossed when every level of one meets every level of the other. For example, if the same two interviewers score candidates in every single week, then interviewer and week cross, and you can isolate an interviewer-by-week interaction. Call a nested factor crossed, and you invent interactions that cannot possibly exist, which quietly corrupts your numbers.

There is a practice dataset for this, the time-to-hire subgroups practice data, with twenty subgroups of five time-to-hire readings each. Plot it as a multi-vari chart and judge by eye which is bigger: the spread of hires within each subgroup, or the drift across the subgroups. You will notice subgroup fifteen sitting noticeably high. The full working is in the written lesson.

Let's check it stuck.

Question one. Name the three families of variation, with the interview example of each. ... Within-unit, the same candidate re-scored differently; between-unit, candidate A versus candidate B; and temporal, this week's cohort versus last week's.

Question two. The three variances are about one and a half, thirteen point four, and six point four. What is the total standard deviation, and why is it not just the three standard deviations added up? ... The total variance is about twenty-one point three, so the total standard deviation is its square root, about four point six. Variances add; standard deviations do not. Adding them up double-counts and overstates the spread.

Question three. Between-candidate variation is sixty-three percent of the total. Is that a problem to fix? ... Usually not. That is likely genuine candidate-to-candidate difference, which is exactly what a scoring process should reveal. Focus instead on the thirty percent temporal drift, which should not be there.

That is module nine. You have learnt to let the process draw its own picture, and to read which family of variation to chase, before spending a single experimental run. Next, we start designing those experiments properly. See you there.
