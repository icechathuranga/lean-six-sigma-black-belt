Module Two. Team leadership and change management.

Here's a truth that catches a lot of new Black Belts off guard. You deliver almost nothing alone. You lead a team you do not manage, through a change people did not ask for, against resistance that is often entirely rational. The statistics in later modules tell you what to change. This module is about getting a group of busy, sceptical people to actually change it, and make it stick. Across our running example, which spans recruiters, hiring managers, I T, payroll and regional HR leads, no amount of regression will help if the regions quietly ignore the new process. Leadership isn't the soft part of Black Belt. It's the part that decides whether the hard part was worth doing.

Start with how teams develop. The psychologist Bruce Tuckman described four stages most teams pass through, and knowing them stops you panicking at the normal rough patch. First, forming: polite, cautious, dependent on the leader; your job is to give clarity, a charter, roles, and a concrete first task. Second, storming: conflict surfaces, over goals, methods, status and workload. This is normal, and even necessary; a team that never storms is usually avoiding the real issues. Your job is to surface the disagreements, keep them about the work and not the people, and reaffirm the shared goal. Third, norming: the team settles, trust grows, people take ownership, and you can step back a little. Fourth, performing: the team works fluidly with little intervention. There's also a fifth stage, adjourning, the deliberate wind-down and recognition when the project ends. The single most important insight here: storming is a stage to lead through, not a sign of failure.

A Black Belt is, first and foremost, a facilitator, someone who helps a group think well together. A few reliable tools. Brainstorming generates many ideas without judging them, keeping idea-generation separate from evaluation. Nominal group technique is for when a few loud voices dominate: each person writes their ideas silently and independently first, then ideas are shared one at a time around the table, then discussed and ranked. It surfaces the quiet people's ideas and cuts down groupthink. Multivoting shrinks a long list fast: give each person a set number of votes to spread across the options, tally them, and the top items survive. And an affinity diagram takes a chaotic wall of sticky notes and has the team silently group related ones into natural clusters, turning a messy brainstorm into a few themes you can act on.

Now the heart of this module: change management, because a better process that nobody adopts is a failed project. Resistance is usually rational from the resister's point of view. Common sources in an HR rollout: fear of loss, like "faster hiring means fewer recruiters are needed"; loss of control or status, like a regional lead who set their own process now following a standard one; lack of understanding, because the case was never made; lack of ability, where they agree but haven't been trained; low trust, because past "improvements" made their work worse; and plain change fatigue, the fifth initiative this year. The response to each is different, which is why you need a model to diagnose which resistance you're actually facing.

That model, for the individual, is ADKAR, from Prosci. It describes the five things one person needs, in order, to change: Awareness of why the change is needed, Desire to support it, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to actually do the new behaviour, and Reinforcement to make it stick. Its real power is as a diagnostic. Take a concrete case. A recruiter understands the new structured interview guide, genuinely supports it, but keeps slipping back to the old way. Where are they stuck? Not awareness, they get the why. Not desire, they're on board. The gap is ability: they haven't had enough hands-on practice. So the answer is training and coaching, not another email explaining why the change matters. Repeating an awareness message to someone whose gap is ability just raises their frustration. Diagnose first, then target the real gap.

Where ADKAR tracks the individual, John Kotter's eight steps guide the whole organisation through change, in sequence: establish a sense of urgency, form a powerful guiding coalition, create a vision, communicate that vision, empower people to act by removing obstacles, create short-term wins, consolidate the gains and push for more, and finally anchor the new approach in the culture. Put the two together for our hiring rollout. Organisationally, you establish urgency with the baseline data showing the regions differ sharply, you build a coalition of the regional leads so it isn't seen as head-office diktat, you communicate a clear vision, you score a short-term win by piloting in one region and publicising the result, then you anchor it in the standard operating procedure. Individually, you use ADKAR to unstick the people who lag. And notice why short-term wins get a step of their own: big change loses momentum without visible early proof, and a publicised quick win sustains support and quietly weakens the sceptics.

Two more duties before we finish. Coaching Green Belts is not doing their project for them. Ask questions rather than give answers, hold them to the method, and let them own both the struggle and the credit. A simple discipline: in a coaching session, aim to talk less than half the time. And stakeholder communication means a plan that tailors the message to each audience, so that no stakeholder ever learns about a change that affects them from someone other than you.

The written lesson has the full roles table, Kotter step by step, and a resistance-mapping exercise. For now, let's check it landed.

Question one. Name Tuckman's four main stages in order, and say which one is commonly mistaken for failure. ... Forming, storming, norming, performing. Storming, the conflict stage, is normal and necessary, not a sign the team has failed.

Question two. A recruiter understands and supports the new process, but keeps doing it the old way. Which ADKAR step is most likely the gap, and what do you do? ... Ability. They lack the skill or the practice, so provide hands-on training and coaching, not more awareness messaging.

Question three. In Kotter's model, why is "create short-term wins" a step of its own? ... Because large change loses momentum and credibility without visible early proof. A publicised quick win, like a successful single-region pilot, sustains support and weakens the sceptics.
