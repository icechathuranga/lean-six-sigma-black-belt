Module Ten. Design of experiments, part one.

Let me start with a frustration you have probably felt. You have done your regression, you have done your multi-vari study, and you have a shortlist of things that seem to drive your process. But seem is the operative word. Both of those tools are observational. They watch the process as it is and spot patterns, and a pattern can fool you, because something else might be quietly moving alongside the thing you are looking at. To actually prove that changing an input changes the output, you have to do something no amount of watching can do. You have to deliberately change the input yourself, hold everything else steady, run the process, and measure what happens. That is a designed experiment. It is the only tool in the whole toolkit that can establish cause.

And the question we are going to answer is beautifully sharp. We have three proposed changes to our hiring process: a structured interview guide, an application pre-screen, and a scheduling tool. Which of them actually cuts time-to-hire? By how much? And here is the subtle part, do they help more, or less, when you combine them? A designed experiment answers all of that from a surprisingly small number of planned runs.

First, a handful of words. A factor is an input you deliberately set, such as the structured guide. A level is a setting of that factor, and here each factor is simply on or off, so two levels: without, and with. The response is the output you measure, which is time-to-hire in days. A run is one execution of the process at one specified combination of settings. Randomisation means doing your runs in a random order, so that some unknown drift, a quiet hiring week, or a new recruiter, does not happen to line up with one of your factors and fool you. Replication means genuinely repeating whole runs, which lets you measure the natural background noise, so you can tell a real effect from a fluke. And blocking means grouping runs that share some nuisance condition, like region or week, so that known, uncontrolled variation is swept out of the comparison instead of muddying it. The slogan is: block what you can, randomise against what you cannot, and replicate to measure what is left.

Now, why bother with a designed experiment at all? Why not just change one thing at a time? That tempting approach is called one factor at a time. Hold everything fixed, switch on the guide, see what happens. Then fix that, switch on the pre-screen, and so on. It has two fatal flaws. First, it is wasteful: you burn runs learning about each factor in isolation. Second, and far worse, it is completely blind to interactions. If the guide helps more when the pre-screen is switched off, changing one thing at a time can never see it, because it only ever wiggles one factor from a single baseline. A factorial design varies everything together in a balanced pattern, so every run pulls its weight for every factor at once, and the interactions reveal themselves.

So here is the design. We have three factors, each at two levels, and we run every possible combination. Three factors at two levels is two to the three, which is eight combinations. We run each one twice, to get our replication, so sixteen runs in total. This is called a two to the three factorial. We write each factor in coded units: minus one for off, plus one for on. When you fit the model, something elegant happens. The intercept comes out as the grand mean of all the data, which here is about thirty days, right at the centre. And each coded coefficient is exactly half of that factor's effect, because the factor travels two coded units as it goes from off to on.

Now the payoff. A main effect is simply how much the response changes, on average, when you flip a factor from off to on. From the data, the structured interview guide is the heavy hitter: on its own it cuts time-to-hire by six point three four days. Call it about six and a third days. The pre-screen saves about two point seven days. And the scheduling tool saves about one and a half days. All three effects are negative, which here is good, because every change reduces the number of days, and smaller is better. All three are statistically significant, comfortably below the usual five percent threshold.

But the really interesting finding is the interaction. An interaction is when the effect of one factor depends on the level of another. Here the guide-by-pre-screen interaction comes out at plus one point four three days, and it is significant. Now watch the sign carefully, because this is a classic trap. On a smaller-is-better response, a positive interaction is not synergy, it is antagonism. The two changes partly do the same job. The guide saves seven point seven six days when the pre-screen is off, but only four point nine one days when the pre-screen is on. So stacking them together gives you less than the sum of their solo savings. That is a genuine planning insight: if you deploy both, budget for diminishing returns, and do not promise the full combined figure.

A couple of plots make this visible. A main-effects plot draws the response at each factor's low and high setting and joins them with a line; a steeper line means a bigger effect, so the guide's line is far steeper than the scheduling tool's. An interaction plot draws the response against one factor with a separate line for each level of the other; and the rule is simple, parallel lines mean no interaction, non-parallel lines mean there is one. For the guide and the pre-screen, the lines are clearly not parallel, they converge, and that converging is the antagonism made visible. A good habit: when an interaction is significant, interpret it before its parent main effects.

How well does the whole model fit? It explains about ninety-six percent of the variation in time-to-hire, which is an excellent fit. Most of the effects are clearly significant. One of them, the pre-screen-by-scheduling interaction, lands just the wrong side of the line, at a p-value of about zero point zero five. The honest call is this: it is not significant at the five percent level, so we do not claim it, but we do flag it for a closer look later. Do not round your way to a conclusion.

The practice dataset for this module is the designed factorial data, the full sixteen runs. Work the effects in a spreadsheet, confirm the guide at minus six point three four, and then, because testing significance by hand is genuinely painful, fit the model in a proper stats tool to get the p-values cleanly. The recommendation writes itself: deploy the guide first, it is the biggest saver; add the pre-screen knowing the two together give diminishing returns; take the small but real gain from the scheduling tool; and re-examine that borderline interaction before you rely on it.

Let's check it stuck.

Question one. How many runs are in a two to the three factorial with two replicates, and what does the replication buy you? ... Eight combinations times two, so sixteen runs. Replication gives you an independent estimate of the natural experimental error, which is exactly what you need in order to test whether each effect is real.

Question two. The guide-by-pre-screen interaction is plus one point four three on a smaller-is-better response. Synergy or antagonism, and what does it mean in practice? ... Antagonism. The guide saves about seven point eight days on its own, but only about four point nine days once the pre-screen is on, so deploying both gives diminishing returns, not the full sum.

Question three. Why does a designed experiment beat changing one factor at a time? ... Because changing one thing at a time can never see an interaction, and it wastes runs. The factorial estimates every main effect from all sixteen runs, and reveals the interactions too.

That is part one of design of experiments. You can now prove cause, size an effect, and spot an interaction. Next time, we tackle the problem of having too many factors, and learn how to get the answer from just a fraction of the runs. See you there.
