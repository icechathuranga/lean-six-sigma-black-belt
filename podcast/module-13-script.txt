Module Thirteen. Advanced statistical process control.

A control chart is a process's heartbeat monitor. Its whole job is to separate two kinds of variation: the ordinary, background wobble that every process always has, which we call common-cause variation, from the unusual, meaningful kind that signals something has genuinely changed, which we call special-cause variation. At Green Belt you learnt to read one chart. At Black Belt, the questions get harder. Which chart is the right one for this data? How should I group it so the chart can actually detect a shift? Why does my chart miss a small, slow drift for months on end, and what catches it faster? Choose the wrong chart, or group the data badly, and you quietly destroy the chart's power, so you end up reacting to noise and missing the real problems.

Every control-chart decision starts with one fork in the road: is your measurement variables data or attributes data? Variables data is a measurement on a continuous scale: days, hours, pounds, a score. Time-to-hire in days is variables data. Attributes data is a count: how many onboarding packs had an error, how many offers were declined. Get this fork right and the rest of the choice almost makes itself.

Variables charts always come in pairs, one for the centre of the process, the average, and one for the spread, the variation, because a process can drift in either one without the other noticing. If you measure one value at a time, say a single monthly figure per region, you use an individuals and moving-range chart, where the gap between consecutive points stands in for the spread. If your data arrives in small subgroups of equal size, say five hires closed each week, you use an average-and-range chart. And for larger subgroups, ten or more, you use an average-and-standard-deviation chart, because at that size the standard deviation is a more faithful measure of spread than the range.

Attributes charts split along similar lines: by whether you are counting defective items or counting defects, and by whether your sample size stays constant. The written lesson has the full selection table; the thing to carry in your head is that the data type decides the chart, and that you use a proportion chart when the sample size varies week to week, because it adjusts its limits each time.

Now the quiet skill that decides everything: rational subgrouping. A subgroup is a small set of measurements you treat as a single point on the chart. The art is to group them so that the variation within a subgroup is only common cause, and the special causes you care about show up between the subgroups. Five hires closed in the same week by the same team make a sensible subgroup: the differences between them are just noise, so a jump from one week to the next really stands out. The golden rule: never let a known source of variation sit inside a subgroup; let it vary between subgroups instead. This is exactly why mixing all four regions into one subgroup wrecks the chart, because region is a known source of variation, and burying it inside the subgroup inflates the spread and widens the limits until real shifts can hide inside them.

Let me work a real example, using the time-to-hire subgroups practice data: twenty weekly subgroups, five hires in each. You compute each subgroup's average and its range, then average those across all twenty. The grand average, which is the centre line of the chart, comes out at about twenty-six point two days. The average range comes out at about four point six days. From those, using the standard constants for a subgroup of five, you get the control limits. On the averages chart, the upper control limit is about twenty-eight point eight days, and the lower control limit is about twenty-three point six days. On the range chart, the upper limit is about nine point six five, and there is no meaningful lower range limit at this subgroup size.

Now read the charts, and always read the range chart first, because if the spread is unstable the limits on the averages chart cannot be trusted. Here, every subgroup's range sits below nine point six five, so the spread is stable, good. Then look at the averages chart, and one point jumps straight out: subgroup fifteen, with an average of twenty-nine point three days, sits above the upper control limit of twenty-eight point eight. That is a special cause. Something specific happened that week, a holiday backlog, a system outage, a policy change, and the chart has flagged it precisely. That matches the dataset's answer key.

Now, a point beyond the upper or lower control limits is the obvious signal, but a healthy process also should not show suspicious patterns. The classic rule sets, Western Electric and Nelson, codify the patterns too unlikely to be mere chance. They divide each half of the chart into three zones and watch for things like two of three points out near the edge on one side, or a long run all on the same side of the centre line, eight in a row in the Western Electric rules, nine in a row in the Nelson rules. The key exam point is simply to name which rule set you are using, because the thresholds differ slightly between them.

Here is a weakness you must understand. A standard control chart, often called a Shewhart chart, judges only the latest point against the limits. That makes it excellent at catching a big, sudden jump, but slow, sometimes months slow, at catching a small, sustained shift of just half a standard deviation. Two special charts fix this by giving the process a memory. The first is the CUSUM chart, where CUSUM is short for cumulative sum: it keeps a running total of how far each point sits from the target, so a persistent small bias quietly accumulates into a visible climb, long before any single point breaks a limit, like adding up small overspends until the total is undeniable. The second is the EWMA chart, which stands for exponentially weighted moving average: it plots a weighted average that leans on the most recent points but never quite forgets the older ones, smoothing out the noise so a gentle drift becomes visible. Both the CUSUM and EWMA charts beat the standard chart for small, sustained shifts, roughly half a standard deviation up to one and a half, because they pool information across time instead of judging each point in isolation. For a big, abrupt shift, though, the plain Shewhart chart is still the fastest.

And at enterprise scale, the trap is false alarms, which across fifty metrics add up into real nuisance. The answer: standardise the metric and the subgrouping so charts are comparable, chart each site on its own limits so a local problem shows up locally, and automate the run-rule checks so humans review signals, not raw numbers.

Let's check it stuck.

Question one. You record one overall time-to-hire figure per region per month. Which control chart? ... An individuals and moving-range chart, because the data are single measurements, not subgroups, so the moving range estimates the short-term variation.

Question two. Why do you check the range chart before the averages chart? ... Because the limits on the averages chart are built from the within-subgroup spread. If the range chart is out of control, that spread is unstable, so the averages limits are untrustworthy and must not be interpreted until the spread is under control.

Question three. A regional time-to-hire drifts up by about half a standard deviation and holds there. Which detects it sooner, a standard chart or a CUSUM chart, and why? ... The CUSUM chart. By accumulating the small deviations from target, it builds a persistent small shift into a clear signal, whereas a standard chart judges each point alone and is slow for shifts under about one and a half standard deviations.

That is advanced statistical process control. You can now choose the right chart, group the data so it can actually see, and reach for a CUSUM or EWMA chart when the drift is small and slow. Next time, we turn to the hardest part of all: making an improvement last, and spreading it. See you there.
