Module Five. Process Capability.

Capability is the one number a Black Belt reaches for when an executive asks the real question. Can this process reliably deliver what the customer asked for? It fuses two voices into a single index. The voice of the process, which is how much the process actually varies, and the voice of the customer, which is the specification limits, the boundaries the customer will accept. Get capability right and you can set a baseline, set a target, and prove improvement. Get it wrong, usually by ignoring the shape of the data, and you'll promise a capability the process can never keep.

Here's the whole idea in one picture. Think of the process spread as a car, and the specification width as a garage. Capability asks: how comfortably does the car fit in the garage? If the garage is three car-widths wide, you can park carelessly and never scrape a wall. Highly capable. If the car barely fits, every single park is a gamble. Now two different things can go wrong. The car can be too wide, meaning the process varies too much. Even parked perfectly in the centre, it won't fit. That is the problem the index called C-P measures, because C-P only compares widths. Or the car can be narrow enough but shoved off to one side, so it scrapes one wall even though it had room to spare. That's a centring problem, and C-P is blind to it. The index that catches it is C-P-K, which measures the distance to the nearest wall. So C-P asks whether the process could fit if it were centred, and C-P-K asks whether it actually does, given where it's really sitting.

Let's put real numbers on it, from our own HR operation. We have a hundred onboarding times, and the customer wants onboarding finished within twenty days. That twenty days is an upper specification limit, a single wall. Faster is better, so there's no meaningful lower wall. The mean onboarding time comes out at about fourteen, and the standard deviation at about one point nine. The capability index is the distance from the average to the wall, measured in units of three standard deviations. Twenty minus fourteen is six days of elbow room, and three standard deviations is about five point seven. Six divided by five point seven is about one point zero five. So C-P-K is about one point zero five. One small note worth saying out loud: we used the overall standard deviation here, the spread of all the data, so strictly this is a long-term index. Either way, the reading is the same. A C-P-K of about one point zero five is only marginally capable. It clears the old floor of one point zero, but it falls short of the one point three three that most customers now expect. There's barely one half-spread of room between the average and the wall. Snug.

There's a sister idea you should keep clearly labelled. The Z-score, or sigma level, is the same distance rescaled into standard-deviation units. For our onboarding data the Z-score is about three point two, and notice it equals roughly three times the C-P-K, three times one point zero five is about three point one five. That link, Z is about three times C-P-K, is worth remembering. And there's a short-term versus long-term twist. Measure a process over one morning and it looks tight. Measure it over three months, across different recruiters, seasons and system updates, and it wobbles more. The tight, one-morning picture gives you C-P and C-P-K, the within or short-term indices. The three-month picture gives you P-P and P-P-K, the overall or long-term indices. P-P-K is almost always the smaller, more honest, customer-facing number, and the gap between them is your prize for stabilising the process.

Now the trap that sinks more capability studies than any other: the normality assumption. The whole method works by laying an imagined bell curve over your data and reading off its far tail to predict defects. But if the real data are lopsided, with a long tail on one side, then the bell you imagined sits in the wrong place, and the sliver of tail you measured is the wrong sliver. You can report a healthy-looking capability and still ship ten times the defects you forecast. So you test the shape. The usual tool is the Shapiro-Wilk test, where the null hypothesis is that the data are normal, so a p-value above zero point zero five lets you treat them as normal. On our onboarding data the p-value is zero point zero three nine, just below the line, with a mild left tail. That's the real-world grey zone. An honest Black Belt reports the tension rather than hiding it. Near-normal, borderline by the test, the index is usable, but I wouldn't quote defect rates to three decimal places.

Now contrast a clearly skewed case. Take a hundred first-response times in hours, strongly right-skewed, where a handful of very slow responses drag out a long tail. Run the Shapiro-Wilk test on the raw data and the p-value comes back below zero point zero zero zero one. Decisively not normal. Running ordinary capability here would be malpractice. You have two valid routes. The first is to transform the data onto a scale where they become normal. A log transform is the natural choice for this kind of multiplicative, right-skewed data, and sure enough, after the log transform the Shapiro p-value jumps to zero point six six, comfortably normal. The more general Box-Cox method agrees, landing on a power essentially equal to the log. The one rule you must not forget: if you transform the data, you must transform the specification limit the same way, or the index is nonsense. The second route is to fit a genuinely skewed distribution, such as a Weibull or a lognormal, and read capability straight off its actual percentiles. And for skewed data, quote the median as the typical value, which here is about four point three hours, not the mean, which the long tail pulls up to about five point six.

One last case. Sometimes there's no measurement to spread out at all, just error or no error on each file. Then capability collapses to one honest question. What fraction goes wrong, and how good is that? You count the defects, turn them into a rate per million opportunities, and convert that rate into the same sigma language, so a pass-or-fail process can be compared like for like with a cycle-time process.

Let's check it stuck. A pause after each question.

Question one. C-P is one point eight, but C-P-K is zero point seven. What's wrong, and what do you fix first? ... The spread is fine, that's the healthy C-P, but the process is badly off-centre, that's the poor C-P-K. Re-centre the mean towards the middle of the specification. Don't attack the variation first.

Question two. Why is P-P-K usually smaller than C-P-K, and what does a big gap between them tell you? ... P-P-K uses the overall, long-term standard deviation, which includes the drift between subgroups that the short-term figure leaves out. A big gap signals an unstable process, and stabilising it is the opportunity.

Question three. Your response times fail the normality test at a p-value below zero point zero zero zero one. Name two valid routes to a capability number. ... One, transform the data to normality, with a log or a Box-Cox, transform the specification the same way, then use the normal maths. Two, fit a skewed distribution such as Weibull or lognormal and use the percentile method.
