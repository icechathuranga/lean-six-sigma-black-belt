Module Eight. Correlation and Regression.

By the time you reach Analyse you have a shortlist of suspected drivers, your Xs, and a problem metric you care about, your Y. Hypothesis tests told you whether a difference exists. Regression goes further. It tells you how much Y moves when an X moves, it lets you weigh several drivers at once, and it hands you an equation that can predict and set targets. For our running example, reducing time-to-hire across a multi-region HR operation, regression answers the question that actually unlocks the project. Of interview rounds, offer delay, and recruiter caseload, which ones genuinely drive the days to hire, and by how much each? That's the bridge from these look related to a defensible improvement plan.

Start with the difference between correlation and regression, because they're cousins, not twins. Correlation measures how tightly two things move together, on a scale from minus one to plus one. In our data, the number of interview rounds and the time-to-hire have a correlation of about zero point six five. Strong and positive. More rounds, more days. But correlation is symmetric and has no units. It tells you nothing about how many days an extra round actually costs, and it treats rounds driving days exactly the same as days driving rounds. Regression is directional and it carries units. It fits a line that predicts Y from X and gives you a slope in days per round. So use correlation to screen which pairs are worth a look, and use regression to model and to predict.

Now fit the line. Simple regression on interview rounds alone gives a slope of about three point eight five days per round, and explains about forty-two percent of the spread in time-to-hire. But days to hire has more than one cause. Multiple regression fits one equation with several drivers at once, and here is the beautiful part: each slope then becomes the effect of that one driver holding the others constant. Fit all three drivers together and the equation reads like this. Predicted time-to-hire equals about nine days, plus about three point four five days for every interview round, plus about one point nine eight days for every day of offer delay, plus about zero point three seven days for every unit of recruiter caseload. Read it plainly. Each extra interview round adds about three and a half days. Each extra day of offer delay adds about two days. Each extra unit of caseload adds about a third of a day. And notice something. The rounds slope slipped from three point eight five on its own down to three point four five once the other drivers were in the model. That small shrink is exactly what controlling for the others looks like.

One warning about that intercept of about nine days. It is the predicted time when every driver is zero at once, which no real requisition ever is, since none has zero rounds and zero caseload. It's a mathematical anchor, not the fastest possible hire. Don't read it as a scenario. And one warning about ranking drivers by the raw slopes. Three point four five for rounds and zero point three seven for caseload are in different units. One extra round is not the same thing as one extra unit of caseload, so you can't call rounds nine times more important just from the numbers. To compare leverage fairly you use standardised coefficients, or you weigh each driver by a realistic change in it.

So how good is the model overall? That's R-squared, the share of the variation in time-to-hire that the model explains. Here it's about zero point six six, so the three drivers together account for about sixty-six percent of why some roles take longer than others. The remaining third is other causes and noise. But R-squared has a flaw: it can only ever rise as you bolt on more predictors, even useless ones. So you also look at the adjusted R-squared, which charges a penalty for each extra driver and only climbs if the new one earns its keep. Here the adjusted figure is about zero point six five, almost the same as the raw zero point six six, and that tiny gap is itself a good sign. It means all three drivers are genuinely pulling their weight; none is dead weight. On top of that, an overall F-test asks whether the model as a whole beats nothing, and it passes overwhelmingly, with a p-value far below zero point zero five, effectively zero. And each driver gets its own t-test, and all three come back highly significant, so every one of them stays in.

Before you trust any of it, you check the residuals, which is just what the model got wrong on each row, the actual minus the predicted. If the model is sound, the residuals should look like structureless noise: no pattern, roughly even spread, roughly bell-shaped. Plot them and if you see a curve, or a fan where the spread grows with the prediction, or a drift over time, the model is missing something. There's a handy mnemonic for the four assumptions, the word LINE. L for linearity, I for independence, N for normal residuals, E for equal variance. There's one more diagnostic worth naming: multicollinearity, which is when two drivers are near-duplicates of each other, so the model can't tell their effects apart and the individual slopes turn unstable. You measure it with the variance inflation factor. A value above five is a concern, above ten is serious. In our data the drivers are almost uncorrelated and every variance inflation factor sits near one, a textbook clean case, which is precisely why the simple and the multiple slopes for rounds barely differed.

Two final cautions, and they matter more than any statistic. First, predict only inside the range the model learned from. Our rounds ran from two to five, so predicting time-to-hire for a role with eight rounds is extrapolation. The line keeps going, but you have no evidence the relationship stays straight out there. Second, and this is the big one, regression shows association, not causation. Even a perfect fit doesn't prove that cutting a round causes faster hiring. Rounds may simply mark senior roles that are slow for other reasons. To claim a true cause-and-effect saving, you need a controlled, randomised experiment, which is exactly where designed experiments, later in the course, will take you.

Let's check it stuck. A pause after each question.

Question one. The rounds slope is three point four five in the full model, but three point eight five on its own. Why the difference, and which do you report? ... The full-model slope is adjusted for offer delay and caseload; it's the effect of rounds holding the others constant. You report three point four five as the driver estimate. The three point eight five quietly includes a little of the other drivers' influence. They're close here only because the drivers are nearly uncorrelated.

Question two. R-squared is zero point six six and the adjusted R-squared is zero point six five. What does that small gap tell you? ... That all three drivers earn their place. The penalty for model size barely dented the figure, so none of them is dead weight.

Question three. The model says rounds strongly predict days to hire. Can you promise the client that cutting a round will save about three and a half days? ... Not from this alone. It's association, and it could be confounded, since senior roles may carry both more rounds and slower processes. You confirm the causal size with a designed experiment before promising the saving.
