Module Fourteen. Sustain, replicate and coach.

Here is an uncomfortable truth: most improvements die quietly. Six months after the celebration, time-to-hire has crept back up, the new checklist is only mostly used, and nobody notices until the numbers are bad again. The Control phase is where a Black Belt truly earns the title, because holding a gain across a multi-region operation is harder than making it in the first place. And there is a second failure that is just as common: a fix that works brilliantly in one region stays stuck there, while three other regions keep paying for the very same problem. This module is about permanence and multiplication: locking a gain in, spreading it, coaching the next wave of improvers, and knowing when a process is so broken that you should design a new one instead.

Start with the control plan. When a project ends, the new way of working lives in people's heads and goodwill, and both of those fade. A control plan converts the improvement into a durable document that answers, for every critical input and output: what we measure, how and how often, what the limits are, who owns it, and, most importantly, what we do when it drifts. That last part is the reaction plan. For our recruitment process, you might chart weekly time-to-hire, owned by the regional talent lead; audit the use of the structured interview guide every month, owned by the hiring managers; and agree that if time-to-hire breaks its upper control limit, a named person reviews that week's bottleneck within forty-eight hours. The reaction plan is the part people skip, and it is the part that actually sustains the gain, because without a pre-agreed response, a red signal just gets explained away.

Wherever you can, prevent the defect rather than monitor for it. That is mistake-proofing, sometimes called poka-yoke. A form that simply cannot be submitted with a missing field prevents the error, rather than catching it later, and prevention needs no reaction plan at all. Always prefer mistake-proofing to monitoring when it is feasible.

At enterprise scale, the control plan stops being a sheet and becomes a system. Fifty teams across four regions need a standard definition of each metric so the numbers are comparable, dashboards that surface only the out-of-control signals rather than raw data, and layered audits, where local leaders check weekly, regional leaders monthly, and the Black Belt or sponsor quarterly. The enemy at scale is drift and divergence, fifty teams each quietly inventing their own version of the standard.

That is where audits come in, and there are two kinds you need together. A process audit asks, are we actually doing what the control plan says, is the structured guide really being used? A results audit asks, is the outcome still good, is time-to-hire still where we left it? You need both, because a process can look perfectly compliant while the results quietly rot, which usually means the metric is being gamed; or the results can look fine while the team has abandoned the new method and is simply coasting on luck that will eventually run out. Layered process audits, the same short checklist run at different levels of the hierarchy at different frequencies, are the standard tool, because they make sustaining everybody's visible job, not the Black Belt's lonely one.

Now the part where a Black Belt's effort really pays off: replication. Say you fixed offer delays in the East region and cut three days. That same root cause almost certainly exists in North, South, and West too. Replication, sometimes called leveraging, is the disciplined spread of a proven fix to other sites. But notice the discipline. You do not re-run the whole project four times, and you do not blindly paste the solution either. You confirm, with a quick diagnostic, that the same root cause is genuinely present at each new site, then you translate the solution to local conditions, different systems, languages, volumes, regulations, pilot it small, and standardise it. Done well, one well-analysed project pays off four, five, even ten times over. Treat replication as a scaled-down version of the whole improvement cycle, and keep a replication register, so the leverage is visible and nothing stalls half-adopted.

Then there is coaching, because a Black Belt's ultimate leverage is not the projects they run but the Green Belts they grow. And coaching is a genuinely different skill from doing the analysis yourself. The core idea is this: your job is to make them think, not to hand them answers. So you coach with questions. What does the data say? How do you know that is the root cause and not just a symptom? What would convince a sceptic? You let them own the project while you protect them from the classic traps, jumping to solutions, trusting a bad measurement system, declaring victory with no control plan. And teaching sharpens you too, because nothing exposes a shaky understanding quite like a Green Belt's innocent "but why?". Beyond any one project, the Black Belt is a change agent, challenging "we have always done it this way", building the business case in the language of the executive, money, risk, and customer, and absorbing the resistance that every change provokes. Technical skill gets you a correct answer; change-agent skill gets that answer adopted.

Finally, when should you stop improving and start designing? The whole improvement cycle you know improves an existing process. But sometimes the process is so far from what customers need, or does not even exist yet, that tuning it is polishing the wrong object. Every process has a ceiling, its best possible performance even with every special cause removed, which we call its entitlement. If even that ceiling still falls short of what customers require, or if you are launching something genuinely new, like a brand-new onboarding programme for a new region, then you switch approaches, from improving a process to designing one, and that is design for six sigma. The idea is to design the quality in from the very start, rather than inspecting and improving it in afterwards.

Design for six sigma has its own roadmap. The most common one has five phases: define, measure, analyse, design, and verify. Define sets the design goals, aligned to business strategy and customer need. Measure captures the voice of the customer and turns it into measurable critical-to-quality requirements. Analyse generates and evaluates design concepts against those requirements and picks the best architecture. Design develops the detailed design, predicts its performance, and builds in robustness. And verify pilots and tests it against the requirements before handing it to operations with a control plan. Use this approach when the process is new, or when an existing process's entitlement simply cannot meet the requirement. The payoff is that quality designed in is far cheaper than quality improved in, but it is slower and more front-loaded, so do not reach for it when an existing process merely needs tuning.

Let's check it stuck.

Question one. What must a single row of a control plan answer, in essence? ... What is measured, how it is measured, how often and on what sample, the control method and its limits, and the reaction plan, plus a named owner.

Question two. Why do you run both process audits and results audits? ... Because a process can look compliant while the results degrade, which signals a gamed or wrong metric; and the results can look fine while the improved process has quietly been abandoned, which is luck about to run out. Each audit catches what the other misses.

Question three. Before replicating the East-region fix to the West, what must you confirm, and why not just copy the solution? ... You confirm, with a quick diagnostic, that the same root cause is present in the West, because a pasted solution aimed at a cause that is not there will fail and discredit the whole method. Then you translate it to local conditions and pilot it.

That is sustain, replicate, and coach. You now know how to make a gain permanent, multiply it across sites, grow the people who will carry it on, and spot when it is time to design fresh rather than improve.
