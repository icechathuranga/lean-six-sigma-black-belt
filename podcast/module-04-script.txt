Module Four. Measurement Systems Analysis.

Here is a trap that catches out even experienced improvers. Every number in your project arrives through a measurement system. Time-to-hire in days, an interview score, a count of onboarding errors, none of them simply float in on their own. Each one is read off some gauge, by some method, by some person, under some conditions. And if that measurement system is noisy or biased, you're polishing a signal you cannot trust. You could spend a whole project chasing variation that lives in the ruler, not in the process. So before you compare regions, before you run a capability study, before you declare any change a success, you first prove the measurement system is good enough to see what you're trying to see. That's the job of Measurement Systems Analysis.

Two words are worth separating at the start. Accuracy is about hitting the true value on average. Precision is about getting the same answer when you measure again. They're independent. A bathroom scale can be beautifully precise, giving you the identical reading every single morning, and still be inaccurate, reading two kilograms heavy every single time. Measurement systems analysis checks both.

Now picture the setup we'll actually use. Three interviewers each score the same ten candidates, from the same recorded interviews, twice each. If the scoring system were perfect, every score for a given candidate would be identical, and the only spread in the whole study would be the real differences between candidates. In reality, three other kinds of wobble creep in. First, the same interviewer, scoring the same candidate twice, disagrees with themselves. We call that repeatability, the noise in the method itself, usually born of a vague rubric or plain fatigue. Second, different interviewers score the same candidate differently. That's reproducibility, one interviewer being systematically harder or softer than another. And third, the candidates genuinely differ. That's part-to-part variation, and it's the one thing we actually want to measure.

The tool that pulls these apart is called Gage R and R, which stands for gauge repeatability and reproducibility. It answers one clean question. Of all the variation you see in the numbers, how much is real differences between candidates, and how much is just measurement error? The preferred engine for it is analysis of variance, because it's the only method that can cleanly separate every source, including the way a particular interviewer treats particular candidates. We'll point you to the written lesson for the full table of formulas. Here, let's just read the result together.

So we run the study. Sixty rows of data: ten candidates, three interviewers, two trials each. The analysis of variance hands back a set of variance components, and here is what matters. The repeatability variance comes out at about three point one. The reproducibility variance comes out at about three point five. Notice that those two are roughly the same size. Hold that thought, because it decides what we fix. The part-to-part variance, the real signal, is far larger, about sixty-two.

Now the headline numbers, and there are two you must never confuse. The first is percent contribution, a ratio of variances, and here it is nine point six percent. That sounds reassuring. But the figure the standards actually judge against is the percent study variation, which is a ratio of standard deviations, the square root of that fraction. Because you're taking the square root of a number below one, it always comes out larger and scarier. Here it is thirty point nine percent, call it about thirty-one percent. And the acceptance rule says under ten percent is good, ten to thirty percent is marginal, and over thirty percent is unacceptable. So thirty-one percent has just tipped over the line, out of the marginal band.

There is one more number to read: the number of distinct categories. It estimates how many genuinely different groups of candidates the system can reliably tell apart. You want five or more. Here it's four. Too coarse.

So the verdict is this. The measurement system is marginal, sitting right on the unacceptable boundary. And here is the diagnostic gift. Remember that repeatability and reproducibility came out about the same size. That tells you both halves need attention, not just one. You can even see the reproducibility half with your own eyes. Interviewer B averages about sixty-nine, while Interviewer C averages about sixty-six, a three-point systematic leniency gap. One interviewer is simply softer than another.

What do you do about it? Two fixes, because both halves are in play. For repeatability, tighten the scoring rubric with concrete, behaviourally anchored descriptions for each band, so that a single interviewer rescoring the same interview lands on the same number. For reproducibility, run a calibration session where all three score the same recorded interviews together and reconcile the gap between B and C. Then re-run the study and confirm that the study variation drops below thirty percent and the number of distinct categories reaches five, before you trust those interview scores in any capability or hypothesis work downstream.

One honest footnote. Gage R and R is a precision study. A gauge can be perfectly repeatable and perfectly reproducible and still be biased, consistently reading high or low. For that you need separate accuracy checks. Bias, measured against a known reference. Linearity, which asks whether that bias changes across the range. And stability, which asks whether it drifts over time. And when your measurement is a category rather than a number, shortlist or reject, pass or fail, variances make no sense, so you switch to attribute agreement analysis and a statistic called the kappa, which measures how much the raters agree beyond what chance alone would hand you.

Let's check it stuck. I'll leave a pause after each question, so you can answer in your head first.

Question one. Your study variation is thirty-one percent, but your percent contribution is only nine point six percent. Which do you report, and why the gap? ... You report the study variation, thirty-one percent. Percent contribution is a ratio of variances; study variation is its square root, a ratio of standard deviations, so it's always the larger number, and it's the one the acceptance bands are written against.

Question two. Repeatability and reproducibility came out about the same size. What does that tell you to do? ... Fix both halves. Tighten the rubric to cut repeatability, and run a calibration session to cut reproducibility. It isn't purely an equipment problem.

Question three. The number of distinct categories is four. In plain terms, what does that mean, and is it good enough? ... It means the system can reliably tell apart only about four groups of candidates. The desired minimum is five, so no. It's too coarse to trust for fine comparisons.
