Module One. Enterprise deployment and project selection.

Let me open with a common way a Six Sigma programme fails, and it isn't bad statistics. It's working hard on the wrong project. A Black Belt is an expensive, scarce resource. Point that resource at a problem that's too small, too vague, or disconnected from what the business actually cares about, and even a flawless project delivers nothing that leadership will ever fund again. So this module is about choosing the right work, and proving its worth in money, before a single measurement is taken.

First, deployment. Deployment is simply how an organisation turns Lean Six Sigma from a handful of enthusiasts into a repeatable capability. A few pieces you should recognise. There's executive sponsorship, a senior leader who owns the programme, sets the goals, and removes obstacles. There are roles. A Master Black Belt sets the methodology and coaches Black Belts; Black Belts lead projects full-time; Green Belts lead smaller ones part-time; a Champion owns the programme's direction and picks projects; and a Sponsor owns an individual project and clears its roadblocks. There's a project pipeline, a continuously topped-up, prioritised list of candidate projects, so that when a Black Belt finishes, the next worthwhile problem is ready and waiting, not invented on the spot. And there's governance, the regular reviews where projects are started, checked, accelerated, or killed.

So how do you choose which problems become projects? Not every problem should. Picture ten candidate problems on a whiteboard. You want the ones that matter to the business, that you can actually finish, and that are the right size. One quick tool is the benefit-effort grid. Plot each candidate on two axes, likely benefit going up, effort to deliver going across. High benefit and low effort, up in the top-left, is where you start. Low benefit and high effort is where projects go to die. The other idea is right-sizing. A project that's too big, like "fix all of HR", will never finish. One that's too small, like "reformat the offer letter", wastes a Black Belt. A good Black Belt project has a problem you can scope in a sentence, a baseline you can measure, and a finish line inside two quarters. That's exactly why our running example is scoped to time-to-hire and onboarding errors, and not "improve HR".

For the shortlist, you get more rigorous with a weighted selection matrix. Each criterion gets a weight, each project gets a score on each criterion, and the weighted sum ranks them. For example, weight strategic fit at five, financial benefit at four, feasibility at three, and data availability at two. If Project A scores a five on strategic fit, a four on financial benefit, a three on feasibility and a four on data availability, you work out five times five, plus four times four, plus three times three, plus two times four, which comes to fifty-eight. Suppose Project B comes out at fifty-seven. They're almost level, and that's the real value of the matrix. It drags a close call into the open, where a sponsor can consciously weigh the tie-breaker, instead of letting a hidden preference decide quietly.

Now the money, told plainly, because a Black Belt must put a credible number on the problem before the project and after it. Keep hard savings and soft savings in separate columns. Hard savings hit the profit-and-loss account: a budget line actually falls, like agency spend that genuinely drops because roles fill faster. Finance can trace it. Soft savings are real but don't directly cut a budget line: time freed up, reduced risk, a better candidate experience. Report both, but never count days saved as cash unless a real cheque stops being written. Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose your credibility.

Let's work the three financial numbers every business case should show. Suppose a project needs a one-off investment of one hundred thousand pounds, and it's expected to deliver sixty thousand pounds of net benefit each year for three years. All of these figures are illustrative. The simple payback period is investment divided by annual benefit: one hundred thousand divided by sixty thousand is about one point six seven years, roughly twenty months. Payback is intuitive, but it ignores the time value of money, and anything beyond the payback point. Return on investment, as annual net benefit over investment, is sixty thousand over one hundred thousand, which is sixty percent a year. Then there's net present value, which fixes payback's blind spot, because money next year is worth less than money today, so future benefits are discounted. At a discount rate of ten percent, with the outlay at the start, those three years of sixty thousand pounds are worth about fifty-four and a half thousand, then about forty-nine and a half thousand, then about forty-five thousand, in today's money. Add them up, subtract the one hundred thousand you put in, and the net present value comes to about forty-nine thousand pounds, positive. A positive net present value means the project earns more than the ten percent hurdle, so it's worth doing. Three numbers, three different questions: how fast do I get my money back, what rate does it return, and is it worth more than its cost of capital.

One more discipline. The business case is only an estimate, and after the project, you prove it. In our running example, a before-and-after study showed mean time-to-hire falling from thirty-one days to twenty-six point six days, a reduction of four point four days, and that difference held up as statistically significant. That measured, four-point-four-day gain is what you translate into validated savings, never the hopeful figure from the original pitch.

And underneath all of this sits one rule: strategy linkage. Voice of the Customer tells you what the customer needs. Voice of the Business tells you what the organisation needs, its profit, cash, growth, risk and compliance. Every project on the pipeline should trace to a stated business objective. If you can't say which corporate goal a project advances, it's probably the wrong project, however interesting it looks.

The written lesson has the full present-value table, the cost-of-poor-quality breakdown, and a selection-matrix template. For now, three checks.

Question one. What's the difference between hard and soft savings? ... Hard savings cut a real budget line that Finance can trace, like reduced agency spend. Soft savings are real but don't directly reduce a budget line, like time freed or risk reduced. Report both, but only count hard savings as cash.

Question two. A project costs one hundred thousand pounds and returns sixty thousand pounds a year. What's the simple payback period, and what does it ignore? ... One hundred thousand divided by sixty thousand, about one point six seven years, or roughly twenty months. It ignores the time value of money, and any benefit beyond the payback point, which is why you also compute net present value.

Question three. Why use a weighted selection matrix instead of just picking the project that feels most valuable? ... Because it makes the criteria, the weights and the trade-offs explicit and defensible, so a sponsor can challenge the logic rather than argue with a hunch, and it exposes close calls that deserve a conscious tie-breaker.
