Module Six. Statistics Foundations.

Everything in the Analyse and Improve phases, every hypothesis test, every analysis of variance, every regression, every control chart, stands on a small handful of ideas. Which shape describes your data, why sample averages behave so beautifully, and how to attach an honest margin of error to a guess. At Green Belt you used these tools. At Black Belt you have to justify them, pick the right one under pressure, and explain to a sceptical sponsor why a sample of forty can speak for a population of forty thousand. This module is the bedrock the next ten modules build on.

Start with the shapes. A distribution is just the shape data make when you pile it up. Toss onboarding times into a histogram and they heap up in a bell. Count onboarding errors per file and you get a lopsided pile near zero. Different processes make different shapes, and each shape has a tidy model behind it that lets you answer how likely is that without collecting a lifetime of data. There are six shapes a Black Belt should know by name. The normal, the familiar symmetric bell, for things like cycle times and scores. The binomial, for counting successes in a fixed number of yes-or-no trials, like offers accepted out of fifty. The Poisson, for counting rare events in a fixed window, like defects per file, where there's no fixed upper limit. Then three that power the tests themselves. The t-distribution, which is the normal's heavier-tailed cousin, used when you've estimated the spread from a small sample. The chi-square distribution, built from squared normals, used for variances and for counts in tables. And the F-distribution, which is a ratio of two variances, the engine that asks whether the spread between groups is bigger than the spread within them.

Now the single most useful piece of magic in all of statistics, the central limit theorem. Here's the trick you can feel in a spreadsheet. Take the first-response-time data, which is badly right-skewed, with a skew of about two point eight. Draw a random sample of a few values and write down the average. Do it again and again, a few hundred times, and histogram those averages. With small samples of five, the pile of averages is already tamer, though it still clearly leans right, its skew down to about one point three. Now push the sample size up to thirty. The lean nearly vanishes, the skew drops to about zero point five, and the averages form a tidy bell, centred on the true mean and far tighter than the raw data ever was. That's the theorem. The distribution of the sample average marches towards a normal bell as the sample grows, even when the raw data never do. In practice, a sample of thirty or more is the common threshold for the approximation to be good. And this is the quiet reason nearly every test you'll run still works on messy, skewed inputs. The tests assume the estimate is normal, not the raw data.

Next, a word that trips people constantly. Standard deviation versus standard error. Standard deviation describes how spread out the individual values are. Standard error describes how spread out the average is. And here's the key law. The standard error shrinks as the square root of the sample size. Which means, and this matters for your budget, that to halve your margin of error you need four times the data, not twice. Quadruple the sample and the error only halves. That square-root law of diminishing returns sits under every interval and every test in the course.

Which brings us to the confidence interval, which is simply honesty about a guess. You sampled a hundred onboarding files and got a mean of about fourteen days. The true mean of all files isn't exactly fourteen. You got close, but not spot on. A confidence interval puts a believable range around your guess. Let's build the real one. The mean is about fourteen, the standard deviation about one point nine, and the sample size is a hundred, so the standard error is one point nine divided by the square root of a hundred, which is about zero point one nine. Multiply that by the t-multiplier for ninety-five percent confidence, which is close to two, and you get a margin of about zero point three eight. So the ninety-five percent confidence interval runs from about thirteen point six days to about fourteen point four days. Read it as: we're ninety-five percent confident the true mean onboarding time lies between roughly thirteen point six and fourteen point four days.

Now, confidence has a precise meaning, and it's easy to get wrong. It does not say there's a ninety-five percent probability that the truth sits inside this one interval. The truth is fixed; it's the interval that varies from sample to sample. What it really says is that if you repeated the whole sampling exercise many times, about ninety-five percent of the intervals you built that way would capture the true value. It's a statement about the long-run reliability of the method, not a bet on this one range.

You can do the same for a proportion. Across four regions, a hundred and fifty-five of two hundred and ten offers were accepted. That's a proportion of about zero point seven four, roughly seventy-four percent. Run it through the interval and you get a range from about sixty-eight percent to about eighty percent. One caution: the simple normal-approximation interval misbehaves when the proportion sits near zero or near one, or when you have very few events. In those cases switch to a better-behaved method, such as the Wilson interval, which is built for exactly that.

Let's check it stuck. A pause after each question.

Question one. The raw response-time data is badly skewed. Why can we still use normal-based tests on its mean? ... Because of the central limit theorem. The sampling distribution of the mean approaches normal as the sample grows, whatever the raw data's shape. The tests assume the estimate is normal, not the raw data.

Question two. Standard deviation versus standard error, one line each. ... Standard deviation is the spread of the individual observations. Standard error is the spread of a sample statistic, like the mean, and it equals the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size.

Question three. You want to halve a confidence interval's width. What has to happen to the sample size? ... It has to roughly quadruple, because the margin scales with one over the square root of the sample size.
