Module Three. Advanced Define.

Here's where projects are quietly won or lost, long before any statistics appear. Define. A Green Belt Define phase scopes one team's problem. A Black Belt Define phase has to hold a cross-functional project together: a charter that several leaders have signed, a map of everyone who can help you or block you, and a disciplined translation of what customers actually want into characteristics you can measure and later engineer. Get Define right, and the rest of DMAIC has firm ground. Get it vague, and you'll spend the whole Analyse phase arguing about scope.

Start with the charter. The charter is the project's contract, and at Black Belt scale it has to survive contact with several departments. It holds the business case, the problem statement, the goal, the scope, the team and sponsor, and the milestones and risks. Two elements deserve special care. The problem statement must be specific, measurable and neutral, with no cause, no blame and no solution baked in. For example: across four regions, mean time-to-hire ranges from twenty-four point five to thirty-two point two days, against an illustrative target of twenty-one days. And the scope must be explicit about what's in, and, just as importantly, what's out. On a multi-region project, a clear "out of scope" list is every bit as valuable as the "in" list. The scaled difference from Green Belt is this: a Black Belt charter is negotiated, not announced. Several stakeholders must agree the scope and the goal before work starts, or they'll relitigate it halfway through.

Which brings us to stakeholders. A stakeholder is anyone affected by the project, or able to affect it. List them, then place each on a power-interest grid: how much influence they have, against how much they care. High power and high interest, like your sponsor and the regional leads, you manage closely. High power but low interest, like a finance director who just wants the numbers right, you keep satisfied. Low power but high interest, like the recruiters doing the new process, you keep informed. And the most dangerous stakeholder of all is the one with high power and currently low interest, ignored, until they suddenly engage and object.

To make accountability unambiguous, use a RACI chart. For each major activity, you assign four roles. Responsible does the work. Accountable owns the outcome and signs it off, and there is exactly one accountable person per activity. Consulted gives input before it's done. Informed is told after it's done. The whole point of the chart is to expose two classic errors: an activity with no accountable person, so nobody owns it, and an activity with two accountable people, so nobody is really accountable.

Now the translation half of Define, which is where advanced Voice of the Customer earns its name. First, be disciplined about who the customer even is. For time-to-hire, the customers include the hiring manager, who wants a good hire fast; the candidate, who wants a quick, respectful process; and the business, which wants the role filled cheaply. Their needs differ, and sometimes conflict. Then you translate each vague verbatim statement, like "the process drags on", into a measurable Critical to Quality characteristic, like "time-to-hire of twenty-one days or fewer", which is again an illustrative target. That Critical to Quality characteristic is the bridge from a feeling to a metric.

Not all customer needs matter in the same way, and the Kano model explains why. Noriaki Kano noticed that satisfaction responds to different features differently. There are must-be features: expected, so their absence makes people angry, but their presence earns no thanks. A candidate expects to be told whether they got the job. There are one-dimensional, or performance, features, where more is simply better: the faster the process, the happier the candidate. And there are attractive features, the delighters: unexpected touches that delight when present but aren't missed when absent, like a personalised welcome pack before day one. Over time, today's delighter decays into tomorrow's basic expectation. The lesson: satisfy the must-bes first, because they're non-negotiable; compete on the performance needs; sprinkle in a delighter; but never trade away a basic to chase a delighter.

Finally, the tool that turns all of this into a decision: Quality Function Deployment, drawn as the House of Quality. It answers a hard question. Given everything customers want, which process changes should we actually make? On the left are the customer needs, each with an importance rating. Along the top are the process changes you could make. In the body, a relationship matrix scores how strongly each change affects each need, using nine for strong, three for moderate, and one for weak.

Let me work a quick column, with illustrative importances and scores. Take three needs: fast time-to-hire, importance five; consistent, fair scoring, importance four; and clear candidate communication, importance three. Now score two possible changes. A structured interview guide scores a three on speed, a nine on fair scoring, and a one on communication. So its column score is five times three, plus four times nine, plus three times one. That's fifteen, plus thirty-six, plus three, which is fifty-four. An automated scheduling tool scores a nine on speed, a one on fair scoring, and a three on communication. Its score is five times nine, plus four times one, plus three times three. That's forty-five, plus four, plus nine, which is fifty-eight. Both changes are worth doing, but notice they serve different needs. The guide mainly buys you consistency, the tool mainly buys you speed. The House of Quality makes that explicit, and stops you funding one change while wrongly assuming it solves everything.

One lovely detail to close on. The Critical to Quality tree you draw in Define, breaking "a fast hiring process" down into drivers like interview rounds, offer delay and recruiter caseload, is really a hypothesis. And later in the course, multiple regression on the course dataset finds all three drivers are genuinely significant, explaining about sixty-six percent of the variation in time-to-hire, with each extra interview round adding about three point four days. So the tree you sketched in Define turns out to be measurably right. That's exactly what a good Critical to Quality tree is for.

The written lesson has the full stakeholder grid, the complete RACI example, the Kano questionnaire, and the full House of Quality with its roof and its basement. For now, three checks.

Question one. In a RACI chart, how many people should be accountable for a single activity, and why? ... Exactly one. More than one means shared accountability, where no single person truly owns the outcome; zero means nobody does.

Question two. Give the three main Kano categories, with an example of each. ... Must-be: the candidate is told the outcome, expected, and its absence angers them. One-dimensional: a faster process, where more is linearly better. Attractive: an unexpected personalised welcome pack, which delights but isn't missed if absent.

Question three. Why must a Black Belt charter be negotiated rather than announced? ... Because a cross-functional project depends on several stakeholders. If they don't agree the scope and the goal up front, they'll reopen them mid-project and stall it.

That's advanced Define, and from here the course turns to the heavy measurement and analysis, starting with validating the data so you can trust every number that follows, and I'll see you there.
