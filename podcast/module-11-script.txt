Module Eleven. Design of experiments, part two.

A full factorial is a wonderful thing, right up until your factor list grows. With five factors at two levels you are already at thirty-two runs. With seven, you are at a hundred and twenty-eight. And remember, in our world each run is a real cohort of real hires; you cannot just re-run it cheaply on a bench. So a Black Belt learns to spend experimental runs strategically: screen many factors with few runs, confirm the vital few, then optimise. This module is the toolkit for that: fractional factorials, screening designs, centre points to catch a hidden curve, and response surface methods to find the best settings.

Let me start with the big idea, the fractional factorial. Instead of running every combination, you run a carefully chosen fraction of them, say half. The saving is real, but it is not free. By running only half the corners, you lose the ability to tell certain effects apart. They get tangled together, and we say they are confounded, or aliased. Their estimates literally add up, and you cannot separate them.

And we can show this happening, exactly, with real data. Take our two to the three experiment from last time and keep only the four corners that form a particular half. Re-estimate the three main effects from that half alone. The structured guide comes out at about minus seven and a half days. The pre-screen at about minus two point nine. And the scheduling tool comes out at minus zero point zero five, essentially nothing. But wait. We know from the full experiment that the scheduling tool's true effect is minus one point four eight days, a real saving. So where did it go? In this half fraction, the scheduling tool is aliased with the guide-by-pre-screen interaction, which was plus one point four three. The number you measure is their sum: minus one point four eight plus one point four three, which is minus zero point zero five. The half-fraction experiment would have told you, confidently and completely wrongly, that the scheduling tool does nothing at all. That is the central lesson of fractional designs, in one real example: you buy fewer runs at the risk of tangled effects.

How do you know, in advance, which effects will be tangled? You design it deliberately, using something called a generator, and from it you work out the alias structure, which effects are confounded with which. There is a property called resolution that summarises how bad the tangling is. Resolution three is the cheapest and the riskiest: main effects get aliased with two-factor interactions, which is exactly what bit us with the scheduling tool. Resolution four keeps the main effects clear of two-factor interactions, though the two-factor interactions are tangled with each other, which is fine for screening. Resolution five keeps everything clear of everything else, which is lovely for modelling, but it costs more runs. Choosing a fraction is really choosing which effects you are willing to confound. That choice rests on the sparsity-of-effects principle, the sensible bet that most high-order interactions are negligible, so aliasing a main effect with a probably-tiny interaction is usually safe. Our dataset is the exception that proves the rule: that guide-by-pre-screen interaction was not tiny, so the resolution-three fraction misled us.

Now, early in a project you might have eight or ten candidate factors and no idea which ones matter. That is what a screening design is for. It uses the fewest runs that can still separate out the main effects, accepting that the interactions get muddled, because you are not modelling yet, you are just sifting the vital few factors from the trivial many. Resolution-three fractional factorials are the classic screening tool. There is also a family called Plackett Burman designs, which let you screen, say, eleven factors in just twelve runs. Their catch is a messy, partial tangling: each main effect is fractionally confounded with many interactions at once, so they are good for spotting the important main effects and poor for estimating interactions. Screen with them, then follow up the survivors with a richer design.

Here is a limitation of every two-level design, and it is easy to miss. A two-level factorial only ever measures the response at the corners, so the surface it fits is essentially a tilted flat plane. It literally cannot see a bend. Suppose time-to-hire improves as you add interview rounds up to a point, and then gets worse again. The corners alone will sail straight past that hump. The fix is centre points: a few extra runs at the mid-setting of every factor. If the average response at the centre is noticeably different from the average at the corners, the surface is curved. Now, this only works for factors you can actually dial to a midpoint, continuous factors, so here we switch to illustrative numbers: interview rounds set at two, three, or four. And these figures are purely illustrative. If the corners average thirty days but the centre runs average twenty-six and a half, that three-and-a-half-day gap is your signal that the flat model is inadequate and you need to allow for a curve.

Once you know there is a curve, and you have narrowed down to two or three continuous factors, you want the actual settings that minimise time-to-hire. That is response surface methodology. Think of it as fitting a smooth, curved contour map over your factor region and finding the bottom of the valley. The workhorse design is the central composite design, which adds star points, extra runs placed out along each factor's axis, to the usual corners and centre points, so you can estimate the curve. You then find the low point mathematically and, crucially, confirm it with a few validation runs, because an optimum from a model is only a prediction until you have tested it. Again, illustrative, but a fitted surface might point to three interview rounds and a particular offer turnaround, predicting around twenty-four days, which you would then go and confirm.

All of this leads to one strategic habit: never pour your whole budget into one giant experiment. Experiments teach you what to do next, so spend in stages. The classic sequence is screen, then characterise, then optimise, then confirm. Screen many factors cheaply to find the vital few. Characterise them with a fuller factorial plus centre points, to get the interactions and check for curvature. If there is curvature, optimise with a response surface design. Then run confirmation trials. A useful rule of thumb is to invest only about a quarter of your runs in that first design, keeping the rest in reserve so you can redirect when a surprise appears. And if a resolution-three screen leaves an important main effect confounded, you can add the mirror-image fraction, called a fold-over, to untangle it, which is exactly the fix our scheduling-tool example would need.

Let's check it stuck.

Question one. In the half fraction, the scheduling tool came out as minus zero point zero five, but the full experiment says minus one point four eight. Why the difference? ... In the half fraction the scheduling tool is aliased with the guide-by-pre-screen interaction, so what you measure is their sum: minus one point four eight plus one point four three, which is minus zero point zero five. Only the full design separates them.

Question two. You have ten candidate factors and you want the vital few fast. Which design family, and what is its limitation? ... A resolution-three fractional, or a Plackett Burman screening design. Few runs, but main effects are confounded with interactions, so it screens main effects only, not interactions.

Question three. Your factorial fits well, but you suspect the best setting is somewhere in the interior. What do you add, and what does it reveal? ... Centre points. Comparing their average with the corners' average tests for curvature, and a big gap means you need a curved, second-order model, which is a response surface design.

That is part two of design of experiments. You can now screen wide, confirm narrow, catch a hidden curve, and home in on the best settings, all while spending your runs like a miser. Next, we go back to Lean, and learn to reason about whole systems of flow. See you there.
