Module Twelve. Advanced Lean for Black Belts.

At Green Belt, Lean was a toolkit: value-stream maps, five S, standard work, a kanban board. At Black Belt, Lean grows up into a way of reasoning about whole systems of flow. Why does adding three more recruiters fail to speed up time-to-hire? Where should the next pound of investment go? This module gives you five powerful lenses: the theory of constraints, quick changeover, total productive maintenance and its key metric, Little's Law, and level loading. Together they stop you from making the single most expensive mistake a Black Belt can make, which is brilliantly optimising the wrong thing.

Start with the theory of constraints, from Eliyahu Goldratt. Its blunt claim is this: every process has at least one constraint, a bottleneck, the slowest step, and that step governs the pace of the whole chain. Improving anything other than the bottleneck is wasted effort. Picture our recruitment pipeline as a relay: screening, phone screen, panel interview, offer, onboarding. Suppose the panel interviews can only handle twenty candidates a week, because the managers' diaries are full, while every other step can handle forty. The output of the whole pipeline is twenty a week, full stop. Now hire more screeners. What happens? You just build a bigger pile of candidates waiting for an interview. Work in progress grows, time-to-hire gets worse, costs rise, and all of it came from improving a step that was not the bottleneck.

The theory of constraints gives you a recipe, the five focusing steps, for attacking the bottleneck in the right order. Step one, identify the constraint, the step with the longest queue in front of it. Here, the panel interviews. Step two, exploit it, which means wringing every drop out of the bottleneck without spending any money: stop panels starting late, pre-brief the interviewers, never let a no-show leave the room idle. Step three, subordinate everything else to it. This is the counter-intuitive one: you deliberately slow the fast steps down, so the screeners release just enough candidates to keep the interviews fed, rather than flooding them. Step four, elevate the constraint, and only now do you spend money: add interviewer capacity, train more panellists, buy the scheduling software. And step five, repeat, because once interviews are no longer the slowest step, the bottleneck has moved somewhere else, perhaps to offer approvals. Go back to step one, and never assume last quarter's bottleneck is still this quarter's.

Once you know where the constraint is, you run the process with a scheme called drum, buffer, rope: the drum is the bottleneck's pace, beating the rhythm for everyone; the buffer is a small protective queue kept just in front of it so it never starves; and the rope ties the release of new work at the front of the line to that pace, so you never dump work in faster than the slowest step can clear it.

The next lens is quick changeover, which came from car factories that learned to change a heavy die in minutes, not hours. In an office, the changeover is the lost time between pieces of work: reconfiguring, waiting, searching, re-briefing. The core move is to split those tasks into internal, the ones you can only do when the activity is stopped, and external, the ones you could do in advance while the previous activity is still running, and then convert as much internal work to external as you can. For a panel interview, the changeover is the gap between candidates: finding the next file, reading it cold, chasing the video link. Make all of that external, pre-load every candidate's pack and link before the session starts, and the panel flows straight on to the next candidate.

The third lens asks whether your resources are actually available when you need them. In a factory, total productive maintenance keeps the machines reliable; in an HR operation, the machines are your applicant-tracking system, your scheduling tool, and your interviewers' booked time. And the single number that tells you how much genuine, good output a resource gives, compared with its theoretical best, is called overall equipment effectiveness. It is the product of three fractions: availability, how much of the planned time it actually ran; performance, how fast it ran compared with its ideal pace; and quality, how much of its output was right first time.

Let me work a quick example, and these figures are illustrative. Take one recruiter's day screening applications. The planned time is eight hours, which is four hundred and eighty minutes. A system outage plus some waiting for approvals cost sixty minutes, so she actually ran for four hundred and twenty. Availability is four hundred and twenty over four hundred and eighty, which is zero point eight seven five. Her ideal pace is one screen every ten minutes, so in four hundred and twenty minutes she could in principle do forty-two; she completed thirty-six. Performance is thirty-six out of that forty-two, about zero point eight six. And of those thirty-six, thirty-three were error-free and three had to be redone, so quality is thirty-three over thirty-six, about zero point nine two. Multiply the three together: zero point eight seven five, times zero point eight six, times zero point nine two, gives about zero point six nine, that is, about sixty-nine percent. Here is the sobering lesson. A recruiter who looked busy all day actually delivered under seventy percent of her potential, and this one number tells you exactly which loss, availability, speed, or rework, cost you the most. And because the three fractions multiply, small losses compound fast.

Now the most useful equation in the whole of flow, Little's Law. It says, very simply, that the work in progress in a system equals its throughput multiplied by its lead time. Let me make it concrete, and again these figures are illustrative. Our pipeline closes twenty hires a week, that is the throughput, and the average time-to-hire is six weeks, that is the lead time. So the average number of open requisitions sitting in the system at any moment is twenty times six, which is a hundred and twenty. Now turn the equation around to find a lever. Lead time equals work in progress divided by throughput. Suppose you cannot easily raise throughput, but you cap the work in progress at, say, eighty live requisitions at once. Then the lead time becomes eighty divided by twenty, which is four weeks. You have cut a third off time-to-hire purely by limiting work in progress, with no new capacity at all. That is why pull systems, with work-in-progress limits, are so powerful.

That brings us to the last idea: levelling the load. Line balancing shares work across the steps so none is badly overloaded while others sit idle, comparing each step's workload with the takt time, the rhythm of customer demand, until every step sits just under it. And heijunka, or level loading, smooths the mix and volume of work over time, so that January's two hundred vacancies do not all crash into screening at once while August's twenty merely trickle in. You level the intake into small, regular batches, and the pipeline runs at a steady, predictable pace. Pull systems only stay stable when the demand feeding them is level, which is why levelling and pull come as a pair: level the load first, then let pull run it.

Let's check it stuck.

Question one. Our pipeline does fifteen hires a week with an average time-to-hire of eight weeks. What is the average work in progress, and what lead time would a work-in-progress cap of ninety give? ... Work in progress is fifteen times eight, which is a hundred and twenty open requisitions. Cap it at ninety, with throughput held at fifteen a week, and the lead time becomes ninety over fifteen, which is six weeks.

Question two. A resource has availability zero point nine, performance zero point eight, and quality zero point nine five. What is its overall equipment effectiveness, and which loss do you tackle first? ... Multiply them: zero point nine, times zero point eight, times zero point nine five, is about zero point six eight, sixty-eight percent. Performance, at zero point eight, is the biggest single loss, so go after the speed losses first, the minor stops and the slow running.

That is advanced Lean. You now reason about the whole system: find the constraint, protect it, and let a level, pulled flow do the work. Next, we return to control charts, and ask the harder questions a Black Belt has to answer. See you there.
