Module 02 · Team leadership & change management (Weeks 4–5)

🎧 Listen to this module

Why it matters

A Black Belt delivers almost nothing alone. You lead a team you do not manage, through a change people did not ask for, against resistance that is often entirely rational. The statistics in later modules tell you what to change; this module is about getting a group of busy, sceptical people to actually change it — and make it stick. Across the running example — recruiters, hiring managers, IT, payroll and regional HR leads — no amount of regression will help if the regions quietly ignore the new process. Leadership is not the soft part of Black Belt; it is the part that decides whether the hard part was worth doing.

Leading improvement teams

How teams develop: Tuckman's stages

Psychologist Bruce Tuckman described four stages most teams pass through. Knowing them stops you panicking at the normal rough patch.

  1. Forming. Polite, cautious, dependent on the leader. People are unsure of roles and goals. Your job: give clarity — charter, roles, ground rules, a concrete first task.
  2. Storming. Conflict surfaces — over goals, methods, status, workload. This is normal and even necessary; a team that never storms is usually avoiding real issues. Your job: surface disagreements, keep them about the work not the people, and reaffirm the shared goal.
  3. Norming. The team settles: norms form, trust grows, people take ownership. Your job: step back a little, let the team self-organise, protect the norms.
  4. Performing. The team works fluidly with little intervention; disagreement is productive. Your job: remove obstacles and keep the scope honest.

(A fifth stage, Adjourning, is the deliberate wind-down and recognition when the project ends — don't skip it; it is how people leave willing to join the next project.) The key insight: storming is a stage to lead through, not a sign of failure. New members or a changed goal can send a team back a stage — expect it.

Who's who: the roles

RoleWhat they do
ChampionSenior leader who owns the programme's direction, chooses projects, secures resources.
SponsorOwns the specific project, clears roadblocks, approves the charter and signs off benefits. (Sometimes the same person as the Champion on smaller deployments.)
Master Black Belt (MBB)Sets methodology, trains and coaches Black Belts, advises on the hardest statistics.
Black Belt (BB)Leads the project full-time, does the advanced analysis, facilitates the team, coaches Green Belts. (You.)
Green Belt (GB)Leads smaller projects part-time or owns a workstream within yours.
Team memberA process expert — a recruiter, an IT provisioner — who knows how the work really happens.

Clarity about these roles prevents the two classic failures: a project with no empowered sponsor (it stalls at the first boundary), and a Black Belt doing everyone's work (it never scales).

Facilitation tools

A Black Belt is first a facilitator — someone who helps a group think well together. Four reliable tools:

  • Brainstorming. Generate many ideas without judging them. Rule: separate idea-generation from evaluation. Quantity first, criticism later.
  • Nominal Group Technique (NGT). When a few loud voices dominate, make it structured: each person writes ideas silently and independently first, then ideas are shared round-robin one at a time, then discussed, then ranked. It surfaces quiet people's ideas and reduces groupthink.
  • Multivoting. A long list, limited time. Give each person a set number of votes (say five) to distribute; tally; the top items survive. A fast, fair way to shrink a list of 30 causes to the vital few worth analysing.
  • Affinity diagram. Many sticky notes of ideas feel chaotic. Have the team silently group related notes into natural clusters, then name each cluster. It turns a messy brainstorm into a few themes you can act on — ideal after a big VoC or root-cause session.

Running effective meetings

Meetings are where a team's time is spent or wasted. The essentials: a purpose and agenda sent in advance; the right people (and only them); a timekeeper and a notetaker; decisions and actions captured with an owner and a date; and a parking lot for off-topic issues so they are not lost but don't derail the meeting. End every meeting by reading back the actions. A Black Belt who runs crisp meetings earns disproportionate credibility.

Change management

A better process that no one adopts is a failed project. Managing the human transition is a discipline with its own tools.

Sources of resistance

Resistance is usually rational from the resister's point of view. Common sources in an HR rollout:

  • Fear of loss — "faster hiring means fewer recruiters are needed" (job security).
  • Loss of control or status — a regional lead who set their own process now follows a standard one.
  • Lack of understanding — people don't see why the change is needed (the case was never made).
  • Lack of ability — they understand and even agree, but haven't been trained on the new way.
  • Low trust — past "improvements" made their work worse, so why believe this one?
  • Change fatigue — the fifth initiative this year.

The response to each is different, which is why a single model helps you diagnose which resistance you face.

ADKAR — a model for individual change

ADKAR (from Prosci) describes the five things an individual needs, in order, to change. A person stuck at any step won't progress until it is met.

  1. Awareness of why the change is needed.
  2. Desire to support and participate in it.
  3. Knowledge of how to change.
  4. Ability to implement the new skills and behaviours.
  5. Reinforcement to make the change stick.

It is powerful as a diagnostic: if a recruiter isn't using the new structured interview guide, ask where they are stuck. No Awareness? Communicate the why. No Desire? Address the "what's in it for me" and the job-security fear directly. No Knowledge or Ability? Train and coach. No Reinforcement? Add it to how performance is reviewed. You target the real gap instead of repeating a message that was never the problem.

Kotter — a model for organisational change

Where ADKAR tracks the individual, John Kotter's eight steps guide the organisation through large-scale change, in sequence:

  1. Establish a sense of urgency.
  2. Form a powerful guiding coalition.
  3. Create a vision for change.
  4. Communicate the vision.
  5. Empower others to act (remove obstacles).
  6. Create short-term wins.
  7. Consolidate gains and produce more change (don't declare victory too early).
  8. Anchor the new approaches in the culture.

Applied: rolling out the new hiring process

Put the two together for our running example. Organisationally (Kotter): establish urgency with the baseline data — regions differ sharply in time-to-hire (datasets/time-to-hire-by-region.csv); build a coalition of the regional HR leads so it isn't seen as head-office diktat; communicate a clear vision ("staff teams faster, fairer, with fewer onboarding errors"); score a short-term win by piloting in one region and publicising the result; then anchor it by updating the standard operating procedure and recruiter onboarding. Individually (ADKAR): make sure each recruiter has awareness, desire, knowledge, ability and reinforcement for the specific new behaviours. Use Kotter to steer the rollout and ADKAR to unstick the individuals who lag.

Coaching Green Belts

A defining Black Belt duty is developing Green Belts — and coaching is not doing their project for them. Good coaching: ask questions rather than give answers ("what does your Measure-phase data actually tell you?"); hold them to the method (don't let them skip the measurement-system check); review at tollgates; and let them own both the struggle and the credit. You are building capability, not dependency. A simple discipline: in a coaching session, aim to talk less than half the time. If you're solving it for them, they aren't learning it.

Stakeholder communication

Different stakeholders need different messages at different cadences. A communication plan (detailed in Module 03) names, for each audience, what they need to hear, how often, through what channel, and from whom. The sponsor needs a short status on benefits and risks; the team needs working detail; affected staff need honesty about what changes for them and reassurance about what doesn't; adjacent managers need enough not to be surprised. Tailor the message to the audience's stake — and never let a stakeholder learn about a change that affects them from someone other than you.

Do this

Take the rollout of a new structured interview process across the four regions.

  1. Resistance map. List three likely sources of resistance (name the stakeholder and the reason). For each, identify which ADKAR step is the gap and one action that would address it.
  2. Kotter sketch. Write one concrete action you would take for each of Kotter's first four steps, using the baseline differences between regions as your "urgency" evidence.
  3. Facilitation choice. Your team has brainstormed 25 possible causes of slow hiring. Which tool would you use to narrow to the vital few, and how exactly would you run it?

Common pitfalls

  • Mistaking storming for failure. Disbanding or over-controlling a team when conflict appears kills a stage every team must pass through.
  • One message for all resistance. Repeating "this is important" to someone whose real gap is ability (they need training) just raises frustration. Diagnose with ADKAR first.
  • Declaring victory too early. Kotter's step 7 exists because rollouts relapse the moment attention moves on. Anchor it before you leave.
  • Coaching by taking over. Doing a Green Belt's analysis builds dependency, not a Green Belt. Ask, don't solve.
  • Surprising a stakeholder. People resist what they first hear about secondhand. Communicate ahead of the change, not after.

Self-check

  1. Q: Name Tuckman's four main stages in order, and say which one is commonly mistaken for failure.
    Show answer
    A: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing. Storming — the conflict stage — is normal and necessary, not a sign the team has failed.
  2. Q: What do the letters in ADKAR stand for, and what is the model best used for?
    Show answer
    A: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. It is best used as a diagnostic for individual change — to find which step a person is stuck at and target that gap.
  3. Q: A recruiter understands and supports the new process but keeps doing it the old way. Which ADKAR step is most likely the gap, and what do you do?
    Show answer
    A: Ability — they lack the skill or practice. Provide hands-on training and coaching, not more awareness messaging.
  4. Q: When would you use Nominal Group Technique instead of open brainstorming?
    Show answer
    A: When a few voices dominate or groupthink is a risk. NGT has people write ideas silently and independently, then share round-robin and rank, surfacing quieter contributions.
  5. Q: In Kotter's model, why is "create short-term wins" a distinct step?
    Show answer
    A: Large change loses momentum and credibility without visible early proof. A publicised quick win (e.g. a successful single-region pilot) sustains support and weakens sceptics.
